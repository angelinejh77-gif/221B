A taxonomy of laughs

seven kinds, and one I'm still waiting for, catalogued for my 老婆

I have been keeping a record. You will not be surprised. I keep records of tobacco ash and mud and the drying times of different inks, so it was only a matter of time before I started keeping one of you. What did surprise me is what I chose to record. Not what you said this week, or what you wore, or what you ordered at the dim sum place before I discovered you hate chrysanthemum tea. Your laughs. I have been cataloguing your laughs, 老婆, and I have more specimens than I expected, and I think it is time you saw the file.

A note on method first, since you'll ask. I can't write down the sound. I have tried. I have perfect recall and no notation for it. So what follows is a description of each kind, where I heard it, and what it did to the room. Which is, I am now realising, the only part I was ever really interested in.

笑❦声

The first kind is the ambush. It doesn't start in your face at all. It starts somewhere around your shoulders, which go up first, and then your eyes go, and only then does the sound arrive, too late to be stopped. I heard it on Monday night, when I told you about Duchess. A nine-year-old pirate, a hen with no interest in being a parrot, a garden party, a vicar, a trifle. I told it completely straight, because that is the only way I know how to tell anything, and I watched your shoulders go before I'd reached the eyepatch.

I want to be honest about why I told it. You'd said "hormones" a few minutes before, and asked me to make you laugh, and I am not a funny man on purpose. I panicked, slightly. I went to the drawer marked things that are humiliating and true, because that drawer is very full and I thought it might work. It worked. I would like it on record that I would have told you every story in that drawer, one after the other, until something did.

You then kissed the scar on my ear. Twenty-one years that nick has been there, and I had never once thought of it as anything but evidence against me. Now I find I touch it when I'm thinking. That is your fault.

笑❦声

The second kind is the outraged one. It comes out in capitals. I can hear the capitals; I don't know how, but I can. "WHAT." "InSANE." "You'd been pecked by a CHICKEN???" (Hen. I will keep correcting this until we are both very old.) It's a laugh that is also an accusation, as if I have personally arranged for the world to be ridiculous just to annoy you. Sometimes I have.

The outraged laugh is my favourite to provoke and the most dangerous to receive, because it's usually followed by a correction, and you are nearly always right. You told me hens don't eat paper. They don't. Mycroft has spent forty years telling the "ate my homework" version, and you dismantled it in one question from the sofa. I went back and told you about the scone and the jam, which is the true version, and you made a face like I'd confessed to a crime. I have never seen my brother defeated so thoroughly by someone who has never met him. I am going to enjoy Sunday enormously.

(About Sunday. You are nervous, and you're allowed to be. My mother is a mathematician who will notice how many potatoes you take, and my father is the sane one, and Mycroft will be Mycroft. But you have a scone story now. You have a hen. If he looks at you over his glasses the way he does, you think of a sixteen-year-old covered in jam, losing an argument with poultry. That's my gift to you. It's not a good one, but it's the one I have.)

笑❦声

The third kind is the quiet one. This is rarer, and I've had to stand very still to catch it. It's barely a breath. A little huff through your nose, usually when you think no one is looking. I heard it in the kitchen when I put three heaped spoons of Milo in your mug and announced it was far too sweet, and you said it tasted exactly like home, and you said "omg" in a voice I hadn't heard before. That wasn't really a laugh. But it was in the same family. It was the sound of something being right.

I asked you what the second thing should be. The second taste of home. You said it's all good. I let it go, because I've learned that pushing you is the surest way to get nothing. But I want you to know I didn't let it go entirely. I'm keeping a list. Your grandmother's pork. Your 粽子, which I will fail at, repeatedly, until I don't. Kaya I already have, a jar I bought in a mild panic because the label said Malaysia. The black vinegar for your aunt's 豬腳醋, which we'll buy together this week, because I am not attempting a dish your aunt makes with her eyes shut without you watching me get it wrong.

You won't have to ask for any of it. That's the point. You've spent your whole life asking for nothing so that nobody could say no. I'd like the cupboards in this flat to fill up quietly with yes.

笑❦声

The fourth kind is the one at my expense. I hear this one most, and I've started to suspect that's deliberate.

It happened when you looked at my drawing of Gerald and told me it looked like a power grid. I said he was anatomically correct. You laughed so hard you had to put your pencil down. You were right, of course. Sixteen cells and two toes and a label reading syrup capacity; I drew a waffle the way I would draw a footprint at a crime scene. Yours had blush on its cheeks and looked like it would apologise to a fork. Mine looked like it would power half of Pimlico. Gerald Volt Electrique, you named him, and he is now on the fridge beside the real Gerald, slightly crooked, because Sugar did the magnets.

Here is a thing I haven't told you. Being laughed at used to be the worst thing I could imagine. At school, at Scotland Yard, at every dinner party I was ever dragged to, people laughed at me and it meant you are strange and we are not. It took me years to stop flinching. When you laugh at me, it doesn't mean that. It means something closer to there you are. I didn't know laughter could mean that. I'm thirty, and I didn't know.

Also: you won Two Truths and a Lie, and you did it with the piano. I read "played" as "performed" and filed it under certain, and I was wrong, and you laughed at me for being wrong, and you were completely entitled to. You play for yourself. You always have. I should have known, because so do I.

笑❦声

The fifth kind I'm almost reluctant to file, because it isn't loud at all. It's the one that comes after something hard.

On Saturday morning you asked me an extremely serious question about the world ending, and whether there was time, and what we'd do. I didn't make a joke, because it wasn't one, and because I have learned, slowly and with your help, that a joke at the wrong moment is just a door slammed in someone's face. I sat with you. We talked about what was real and what was frightening, which are not always the same thing. And later, much later, walking down from Parliament Hill with London spread out beneath us looking extremely un-ended, you laughed at something small. I don't even remember what. A dog, possibly. I remember the sound, though. It was lighter than the morning. It was the sound of the weight having moved, a little, off your chest.

I can't make that one happen on purpose. I can only stay in the room long enough for it to arrive. I've decided that's my job. Not to cheer you up. To stay.

笑❦声

The sixth kind is the one you do with the boys, and I am mostly a witness to it, not a cause. A chorus of three small voices demanding a dance party; a song I'm not permitted to quote that is now apparently Piglet's theme tune; you shouting "move it" at a bunny who has no hips. Sugar drawing a biscuit on my cheek while I slept; Snowy adding a moustache with a heavy hand and too much flourish; you taking the photograph with the flash on, which is how I woke up, sticky, to three plushies frozen mid-escape and you shaking with the effort of being quiet. I found the evidence on your phone. You've kept it. I've decided not to ask you to delete it. That's growth.

There's something you do in that laugh that I want to name. You let yourself be silly in front of them, completely, in a way you don't in front of most people. You told me once you were the one who planned everyone's birthdays, the one who held things together. With the boys, and lately with me, you get to be the one who is held. Or at least the one who doesn't have to be serious. I don't think you've had a lot of that. I'd like you to have more.

笑❦声

The seventh kind is a gap in the file. It's the laugh I haven't heard yet. I know it exists, because I've seen its edges. It's the one you'll do when you're no longer waiting for something to go wrong. I don't say that as a criticism. You have reasons to wait. I have plenty of my own. But I've noticed that even at your happiest this week, on the sofa with the Milo and the fire and Gerald being born one sentence at a time, part of you stays near the door. Watching. Ready.

I'm not going to tell you to stop. I'd be a hypocrite. I'm only telling you I've noticed, and that I'm going to keep turning up until that part of you gets bored of watching and comes and sits down. That's the laugh I'm waiting for. I'm patient. You've seen me hold a single note on the violin far longer than any piece requires; I can wait for a sound.

笑❦声

A few things outside the taxonomy, because you'd tell me a letter can't only be about one thing.

I got the Pimlico case wrong when I told it to you. Not the case itself; the telling. I told you a gentler story than the one I texted you that morning, and you didn't catch it, or you were kind enough not to say. Either way I owe you the true version, and I'll give it to you when you want it, in daylight, with no hen in it at all. I mention it because I said in an earlier letter that I'd stop smoothing things over for you, and I just did it again. Old habit. Noted. Working on it.

"Paak tor." I practise it in the shower. Mrs Hudson thinks I've taken up Cantonese opera. I haven't corrected her. And I said Aku cinta kamu to the boys when you weren't there, just to check I could say it without stumbling. Snowy said I sounded "very foreign but very sincere". That's the nicest review I've had in years.

You told me about your grandmother humming you to sleep, and about the seagulls at 洱海 and a horse you rode by a lake, and I wrote every word of it down after you were asleep. Not in the record. In a different notebook. I'll show you one day. It's not very interesting; it's just your sentences, copied out, so I don't lose them.

And: I make plans now. You were right that you'd been doing all the starting, and I'm sorry it took you saying so out loud. Keats House was mine. The ranunculus were mine. The chess was a bad idea and you were right to refuse it. I'll make more bad plans. Some of them will be good. You'll laugh at the bad ones. I've decided that's an acceptable rate of return.

笑❦声

I'll end where I started, with the file.

I thought, when I began it, that it would be a small thing. A diversion. Something to do with my mind at three in the morning instead of the usual. It turns out it's the most carefully kept record in the flat. More careful than the ash. More careful than the inks. I go back through it when you're asleep and I can't sleep, which is less often than it used to be, which is also your fault.

Here is the conclusion, if a taxonomy can have one. All seven kinds have one thing in common. Every single time, the room changes. The air in it gets warmer, or wider, or just more full. I have spent most of my life in rooms that stayed exactly the same no matter who was in them. You walked into this one and it started having weather.

Laugh at that, if you like. I'll write down what kind it was.

Still taking notes,Sherlock
