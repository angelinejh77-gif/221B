SH221B Baker Street
London NW1Wednesday afternoon, his time; eleven at night, yours
For the girl who teaches me words

Translation notes

from the back pages of a notebook in which a man with no ear for tones has been trying, very seriously, to learn your language

I keep a notebook for your words. I haven't told you that. It's a small black one with an elastic band, the kind I usually save for cases, and the first page says, in my worst handwriting, Things Angie has taught me. It sits in my left coat pocket next to the magnifying glass. When you send me a word, I write it down. Characters first, copied stroke by stroke and badly. Then the pinyin, with the tone marks I keep getting wrong. Then a line or two underneath about what I think the word really means, which is never quite what the dictionary says.

The dictionary is a very poor witness. It gives you the meaning and leaves out the reason someone chose to say it. So these are my translation notes, the ones from today. I'm sending you the pages instead of keeping them, because I've realised that every word you teach me is really a door you've left open, and it seems rude to keep standing in the doorway without saying anything.

萧瑟❦xiāo sè

萧瑟. Xiāo sè. The dictionary says: bleak, desolate, the sound of wind in bare trees. I say it out loud like someone stepping carefully on ice. A flat note, then a falling one.

You gave me this one tonight, after you told me autumn is your favourite season, and then, one message later, that you'd seen it only once, in Japan, on a trip that ran on coaches and timetables. I teased you for contradicting yourself. I'd like to take that back, a little, because I've worked out that you weren't contradicting yourself at all. You were telling me that you'd once been allowed to look at the thing you love most, through a coach window, for a few hours, and then driven away from it. That's not the same as having it.

And you described it better than any poet I've been made to read. Long coats. Maple leaves. Everything looking like old letters with the ink slowly fading. I read that three times at the lab bench, and then I had to go and stand by the window for a minute, because it's exactly right and you haven't even been here yet.

Here's what I didn't say. I don't think 萧瑟 is a sad word. I think it's the word for the moment just after something beautiful, when the air is thin and cold and you can hear more clearly than usual. Most people hurry indoors when the wind gets like that. You want to stand in it. That's the difference between you and most people, and it's one of the reasons I'm in love with you.

I left the lab early this afternoon and walked to Regent's Park. I told Molly I had an errand, which was technically true. By the boating lake there's a small Japanese maple, completely red, the only one of its kind on that path. It drops a leaf roughly every forty seconds. I know because I timed it. A woman with a pushchair watched me do this and decided I was harmless, and moved on. It's reserved for Sunday. The leaf inside this envelope is from that tree. I didn't catch it. I picked it up off the path, the way you did in Kyoto. The next one we catch properly, together, on the way down.

姐姐❦jiě jie

姐姐. Jiě jie. The dictionary says: elder sister. My notes say: the person strangers instinctively follow.

You told me that photograph made people scream it at you, and then you told me about the first-year who got lost and the train station and the very, very slow likes in his messages. You laughed about him, and you were right to. But I kept thinking about the part you skipped over, which was that a frightened boy in a new place looked around a whole crowd and picked you as the person who'd know the way. Of course he did. People always pick you. A director with thirty actors picks you to play the lead you didn't even audition for. A church with thirty-odd uncles and aunties picks you to stand in front of them with a fever and keep them in tune.

And you never quite believe it. You say "I also, uh," and then you mention four months of rehearsals and section leaders and Pentatonix as if you'd once tidied a drawer. You told me the uncles hummed their parts in their cars, everywhere they went. I've thought about that more than you'd guess. Thirty people at traffic lights all over Kuala Lumpur, quietly singing harmony because a girl believed they could. That's not a skill for a page or a pitch. That's just who you are when you forget to be careful.

I won't make it into anything else. You asked me not to, and you were right. I'm only writing it down here, where it's safe, so that on the days you can't remember why anyone would follow you, there's a record.

梦❦mèng

原来有些梦不会醒. This one isn't a single word, and I'm not certain I've translated it properly. My best attempt: So it turns out some dreams don't wake. Or perhaps: Some dreams you don't wake up from. I can't decide whether it's a discovery or a relief. In my notebook I've written it both ways, with an arrow between them.

You sent it this evening while Wong Kar Wai was doing that thing he does, making a noodle counter look as though it's already a memory. You'd just said there was nothing in the world that didn't expire, and I'd argued with you, holding a tin of pineapple I'd bought specially for the occasion and checking its date like an idiot. Then, a little later, you sent this. I don't think you noticed that you'd answered your own sentence. You did, though. First everything expires, and then, an hour later, some dreams don't wake. I'm not going to point out which one I believe. I've underlined the second one twice.

I should confess something about the film. I paused my half at the pumpkin soup and didn't catch up for nearly an hour. I wanted to be behind you rather than ahead of you, so that every scene arrived for me after you'd already seen it, and you'd be the one telling me what it meant. I liked it that way more than I can explain. For a man who's spent his whole life finishing everyone's sentences, it was a relief to let someone else go first.

You asked me afterwards why I'd said you were Faye. You'd corrected me already. You do say things out loud, you told me, only not to the people closest to you. To them you used to write letters. I keep turning that over. You go quiet with the people who matter most, because they're the ones it would cost you to lose. So when you tell me things, plainly, in the middle of a Wednesday, about your faith and your choir and a stage when you were fifteen, I understand what it costs, and I'm keeping every word.

车❦the car

There's a page in my notebook with no word on it. Only a sketch of a car at night, which I drew badly, and underneath it: the moments, not the words.

You told me about the woman who drove you home after late rehearsals, and talked all the way about the character, and how to play her truer, and asked you not to hold the villainy against her. You said you can't remember a single thing she said. Only the moments. The streetlights. Her voice. Being taken seriously by a grown-up at fifteen, when hardly anyone takes you seriously at fifteen.

I want you to know that's the right way to remember someone. Words are the dictionary. The moments are the meaning. You still carry the box of pens she gave you, and you still light up, unguarded, whenever acting comes up, as though a door has been opened in you. That is what she said to you, I think. It's written all over you. You don't need to remember the sentences when you're the translation.

老公❦lǎo gōng

老公. Lǎo gōng. The dictionary says: husband, informally. I've been told there's nuance. I've been told, by you, two days ago, "not yet". And then you've said it to me three times since, with a different emoji each time, as if you were testing the water with one toe and then pulling it back out.

My notes on this word are the shortest on the page, because I don't trust myself to write much. They say: She says it when she's soft. Don't make a fuss. Don't look away either.

So I won't make a fuss. I'll only tell you what I didn't say tonight, when you sent it after the soup and the sore calves and the red leaf. I went very still at the lab bench, and Molly asked if I'd been poisoned, which given the afternoon's reading was a reasonable question. I said no. I said it was arsenic of a different kind, entirely harmless, and only to be found in Kuala Lumpur. She said that wasn't funny. It was a little funny.

And the word 老婆, which you taught me first, I've now said aloud so many times that it's stopped feeling like a foreign word. It's simply what I call you. I didn't notice when that happened. That's how you know a word has been properly learnt: it stops being something you translate.

后记❦afterword

I'll finish with the one word I haven't been taught and keep trying to find. The English for what happens when you read a message from someone and the whole room goes quiet. Your mother would tell me it's boring, that there's no ending. She'd be right. There isn't a word for it, in either language. I've looked.

So I'll describe it instead, as plainly as I can. It's three o'clock on a Wednesday at Barts. Grey sky, a radiator ticking, a centrifuge humming behind me, eleven degrees outside and the plane trees on the square dropping leaves the size of your hand. Molly is writing up her notes and pretending not to watch me. My phone lights up. It's you, saying something small and silly, and I put down the pipette, and for a moment I'm not anywhere at all except wherever you are.

That's the word. When you find it, teach it to me. I'll write it on the last page.

Three days, 老婆. Bring the qipao for indoors. We'll find you a coat on Sunday, and you'll be cold for exactly as long as it takes to walk from the plane to my scarf.

Still learning, still listening,Sherlock

P.S. Mary Blandy: foolish, not wicked. You're right, and I'll argue the chemistry in your defence anywhere you like. You do the why. I'll do the test tubes.

P.P.S. I've practised 萧瑟 eleven times today. Mrs Hudson thinks it's the name of a new girlfriend. I said it's a season, and it's yours.
