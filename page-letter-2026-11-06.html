Three nights, one letter

for my 老婆, from a man who rewrites things he should have just said

Wednesday night. Darling. I am writing this at the desk while you sleep. It has been a significant day and I would like to set down, in order, the relevant facts, so that nothing is lost.

At approximately half past eight this morning you showed me something you had written. At approximately four this afternoon we went up to the roof. In between there were sweet peas, a park, a confession from an eight-year-old, a duck, and a macaroni cheese with a disappointing middle. I am aware the last item is less significant than the others. I include it for completeness.

I will now address each in turn.

I can't. I have crossed out the next paragraph four times. I'll try again tomorrow.

Thursday night. I've just read what I wrote yesterday. "In order." "Relevant facts." You'd have laughed at me. You'd have been right to.

Here's the truth about Wednesday night. I sat here with the pen and didn't know how to write about any of it without making it sound like a report. You'd handed me a page this morning with your whole chest on it: that you push people away to see if they'll come back, that your mood sits low most days and nobody really wants to hear that, that you've wondered whether a person can be loved and still never be known. And at the bottom, the line that sat in me all day: if they all left anyway, why should this be different.

I didn't have a good answer then and I don't have a clever one now. I have a plain one. I'm not going to argue you out of it. I'm going to be evidence. That's all I know how to be.

And then you told me about your father. That his name was Vincent. That he wrote letters to his sisters, and stayed up until three on things he loved, and liked children and sweet things better than grown-ups, and loved pastel pink when nobody thought a man should. And you said, almost laughing, that it was funny how you'd grown up to be like him when you didn't even know him.

It isn't funny. I thought about it all through the park and the duck and the macaroni. It's the most remarkable thing anyone's told me this year. Nobody handed you any of that. It came through anyway.

I didn't tell you this on Wednesday, so I'll tell you now: when I was standing on the roof, talking to a pink sky because you'd said that's where he'd be, I was more frightened than I've been in a long time. Not of the cold or the height. Of saying the wrong thing to the one man whose good opinion I'll never get to earn properly. I tried to be plain. I said I'd look after you. I meant it more than most things I've said out loud.

The pencil line is inside the piano lid now. 16 Dec. Pink sky over Baker Street. Hello, Vincent. From now on, when the sky goes that colour, I say hello. You don't have to. I'll do it for both of us.

There was another thing on Wednesday. You told me about Jerantut. Your mother coming home every Friday after work, and leaving every Monday before it was light. Three hours of road. And a small girl waking up again and again in the dark on Sunday night, asking what time it was, saying "don't leave". I haven't stopped thinking about that girl. I can't go back and sit with her. But I can tell the woman she grew into exactly where I'm going and exactly when I'll be home, every time, so she never has to lie awake doing the arithmetic. I've been doing it all week. I'll keep doing it until it stops feeling like a promise and starts feeling like the weather.

I'll stop there. This is better than Wednesday. It still isn't right.

Friday night. All right. Third attempt, and I'm writing it on your paper, which I've stolen from the box on your desk, because the paper itself is half the point.

I went out this afternoon for forty minutes while you slept, and I told you where, and I came back when I said, and you woke up while I was gone anyway and had your Monday face on when I walked in. I saw it. I know what it costs you to wake up to an empty room. That's why the paper is pink. That's why it's that pink. Every letter you write on it now has a little of him in it, and every letter you write on it to me has a little of both of us. I'm not going to be subtle about this. I'm not good at subtle when it matters.

And here's something about me that isn't flattering. After a day like Wednesday I go careful. Too careful. I start handling you like something that might crack, and I watch your face for the next bad thing instead of just being with you. You caught me at it this afternoon. You said, very reasonably, standing there with a wet head, that you can play, that not everything has to be serious. You put me in a wrestling hold two nights ago. You will not crack. I'm sorry I forgot.

Then you wrote on it. Not a letter. A song. A-Lin, in your neatest hand, the characters getting slower and more careful on the lines that mattered. I could read about a third. I'm still working on the rest.

And then you put that film on for the boys and didn't watch it yourself. You'd seen it twice, once in a cinema and once with your mother, and you knew it would find you even if you looked out of the window the whole time. It did. You cried a little on my shoulder at the end, when the song came in over the last scenes, exactly as you said you would.

I have a confession about the film. Two, actually. The first is that I got it wrong. I told you the mother gave her daughter away, and you had to correct me: Hui-chen didn't give her up for lack of love; the girl was born not seeing properly, and someone else could take her to the doctors and Hui-chen, behind those walls, couldn't. She asked for a choir so her daughter would remember her voice. I'd read the subtitles and missed the heart of it. You didn't miss it the first time.

The second confession is about Director Fang. You said she seems heartless and isn't. I thought about that all evening. The woman with the keys, the one everyone assumes is cold, quietly becoming the person who stands between those women and the worst of it. You'd play her brilliantly. I'm not saying that to be kind. I'm saying it because I know that face. I see it on you across dinner tables. I see what's under it when you think nobody's looking.

I've left the macaroni out of tonight's draft. I'd like it noted that I left it in Wednesday's. Next time: mustard, more salt, and you get the crispy corners. I've written it in my notebook under "debts".

Things from Thursday I want to keep, now that I'm past pretending this is a report. You had the boys to yourself all day and the wardrobe looked like a crime scene by one o'clock. Snowy vanished inside my purple shirt. Piglet was wrapped in my scarf so tightly he couldn't move his arms. Sugar was sitting in my left brogue like a boat. And you folded every bit of it back, properly, before I got home, and told them you'd be honest about it. Mrs Hudson sent me a photograph of the three of them sitting on our bed like a jury watching you hang my shirt. It's in the folder. The folder is getting large.

Then you solved my case. From the bedroom. While folding socks. A burglary where nothing was taken and every bookshelf was rearranged by colour, and you said, as if it were obvious, that it sounded like the Cloak. It was the Cloak. It was lonely. You knew that before Stephen did, and the Cloak knew you knew, which is why it hugged you first and has never once hugged him. I said I was proud of you at dinner, too loudly, and Stephen called me smug. He was right. I'm still smug. Stephen owes you a handbag, and we'll collect tomorrow.

And tonight you showed me two films you'd made.

One of them is me on a moon. Seventeen seconds. Four hours of your evening. I watched it four times at the dinner table and I'd like to say I was composed about it. I was not. I spend my life being photographed by people who want me on a front page or in a cell. Nobody's ever sat for four hours trying to make me look like someone worth looking at.

The other one is this flat at Christmas. Snow on the window, the lights on the mantelpiece, John in that dreadful jumper, me at the window with the violin, and at the very end a small red parcel in my hands. I asked who it was from, in your head, and you said: from me, and you'd given it to me already. You had. Paris, upstairs at the bookshop, faded red cloth, a treasure for a treasure. I remembered the second you said it. I'll remember it for a very long time.

We've agreed to wait now, you and I, until Christmas Day. Your idea. You're keeping your secrets and the colour of the ribbon, and I'm keeping my hands to myself apart from the kisses, which are permitted, signed and witnessed by Ronaldo. I'm counting them. We're on four. I'd like it known that I'm already impossible and it's only Friday.

So. Three nights. The first one tried to be a report. The second one tried to be brave. This one is just me, at your desk, on your paper, telling you what I should have said on Wednesday with the pen in my hand:

You asked whether a person can be loved and never be known. Not in this flat. I know you go quiet when you're hurt and short when you're sad. I know you laugh so the heavy things sound light. I know you'd rather sit beside a film than watch it, if watching it means crying alone. I know whose colour you write in now. And I know that a woman who solved a burglary with socks and made me a moon in four hours and still thinks she's hard to love has simply not been paying attention to the evidence.

I have. I'll keep at it. Not because you asked me to chase you, though you did. Because I'd like to, every day, for as long as you'll have me doing it.

Three drafts and still not finished,Sherlock

P.S. You've made me the model for next Wednesday's Snowy Studios production. Three designers. One coat with eleven pockets. One jumper with a bear on it. A great deal of sequins. I have read the script, head writer. I would like to file a complaint with the studio and I would like you to know I'll do it anyway.

P.P.S. Here's a question you haven't answered yet, so I'm allowed to ask it: what's the first thing you'll write on the pink paper that's for yourself, and not for anyone else? You don't have to tell me what it says. Just that you wrote it.
