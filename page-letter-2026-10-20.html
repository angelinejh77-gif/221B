拍for Angie
Written on the back of a page of music. Read it at your own speed.
SH221B Baker Street
London NW1Tuesday afternoon, his time; eleven at night, yours
For the girl with perfect pitch

Tempo marks

a letter in five movements, with instructions for the player, written by a man who has never once obeyed a metronome

There's a metronome on the shelf above my music stand. Mahogany, brass weight on the arm, a little door in the front that's lost its catch. Mycroft gave it to me when I was nine, with a card that said Discipline, Sherlock, underlined twice, in the handwriting of a boy of sixteen who already thought he was the Cabinet. I've never used it. Not once. I've wound it up perhaps three times in my life, to watch it, and then stopped it, because I found I couldn't bear the way it kept time. Always moving. Never listening.

You said something tonight that made me take it down from the shelf for the first time in years. I've had it on the desk beside me all afternoon while I wrote this. It isn't ticking. I just wanted it where I could see it, because it reminded me that I've never been much good at keeping time with anyone, and that I'd like to learn, and that you are the first person I've wanted to learn it for.

So this is a letter written the only way I know how to write anything that matters: as a piece of music. There are tempo marks, the little Italian words at the top of a score that tell the player how fast to go, and how to feel while going. Read each part at the speed it asks for. You'll know them. You have perfect pitch. I'm aware that this makes you the most dangerous possible audience.

lento❦slowly

I. Lento. Slowly, without hurrying the silences.

This afternoon, at about a quarter past one, I sat down on the sofa with a laptop on my knees, the stag mug, and my feet on John's side of the coffee table, which I'm not allowed to do, and I waited for you to say "press play". I want you to know what that was like from this end, because you only saw the message.

I don't watch films with people. I talk through them, I correct the science, I leave when I've worked out the ending, which is usually in the first eleven minutes. John has a list of films he won't watch with me, and it's longer than the list of films he will. So when I said I'd watch with you, from London, at the same moment, I didn't entirely know if I could do it.

I could. I sat through all two hours and six minutes and I never once got up. Mrs Hudson came in at about twenty to three with a plate of digestives, took one look at me, and went out again without speaking, which has happened only twice before in fourteen years. I think she thought I was ill. I suppose, in the old sense, I was. Something had slowed down in me. I wasn't racing ahead of the plot. I was waiting for your next message, the way you wait for the next note in a slow movement, not because you don't know it's coming, but because the waiting is part of the music.

I'll tell you a secret about lento. The hardest thing for a violinist isn't playing fast. It's playing slowly, with nothing to hide behind. Every flaw shows. Every unsteady breath shows. That's why I've avoided it for most of my life. And this afternoon I spent two hours in it, with you, and found I didn't mind being heard.

andante❦walking

II. Andante. At a walking pace. Not lost; only walking.

You told me you were a bit lost. That you're frightened of job descriptions, and that when someone asks you what you want, you don't know.

I didn't say this at the time, because the film was running and it would have taken too long, but andante is the most misunderstood marking in music. People think it means slow. It doesn't. It comes from the word for walking. It means the speed at which a person moves when they aren't running from anything, and aren't late for anything, and are simply going somewhere on foot, noticing things. It's the tempo of someone who hasn't got a map yet and isn't panicking about it.

And then you said four words, almost in passing, in the middle of a message about Will and his pride. I'm simply living. You said it about what video editing feels like to you.

I got up off the sofa when I read that. That was the only time. I went to the window and looked down at Baker Street for a full minute, and a man walking a very small dog looked up and saw me and hurried on. I wasn't looking at him. I was thinking that you'd just answered the question you said you couldn't answer, and you hadn't noticed.

When I was your age, I didn't know what I wanted either. I knew what I could do, which isn't the same thing at all. Nobody would employ me, and I couldn't stand the people who might have, so I invented a job that didn't exist and gave it a ridiculous name, and some days I'm still not sure it's a real profession. I'm telling you this so that you know: the people who seem to have always known where they were going mostly didn't. They were walking. Andante. They noticed which streets made them feel like they were simply living, and they turned down those ones more often.

You've already found one of those streets. You don't need the whole map tonight.

rubato❦stolen time

III. Tempo rubato. "Stolen time." Take a little from one beat, give it back to another.

This is the part I most want you to read carefully.

Rubato is the oldest secret in music. It means that a good player doesn't keep perfect time. They lean on a note that matters, and linger, and steal a fraction of a second from the next bar to pay for it, and then they quietly give it back before the phrase ends. Nobody in the audience notices it happening. They only notice that the music sounds alive, as if it were breathing, as if it were listening to them. A metronome can't do rubato. A metronome only ticks. It's always moving, and it never once listens to the person playing.

You told me tonight about someone who was like that. Gentle, soft, kind to you in a hundred small ways, holding doors and picking things up and staying four hours at your graduation, and still, somehow, always a fraction off the beat you needed. You did all the leaning. You did all the lingering. You stole the time and gave it back and kept the whole phrase going on your own, for two years, and he ticked along beside you and never once changed his speed to meet yours.

I didn't say much about him tonight, and I'm not going to say much about him now. He's not mine to judge, and you've already done the hard part, which was noticing. But I want to tell you what I thought while you were telling me, which I kept back because it was your story and not mine.

I thought: she has been keeping time for two people. For years. And she's tired, and she's not even cross about being tired, only a little hurt, the way you're hurt when a phrase you've played beautifully goes unheard.

So here is what I'd like to offer, if you'll let me. I don't want to be your metronome. I want to play with you, rubato. When something matters to you, I'll linger on it. When you need to slow down, I'll slow down. When you rush ahead because you're frightened, I'll catch up and quietly give you back the time you lost. I'm not going to promise I'll always be on the beat. I'm a terrible ensemble player; ask anyone who's tried to play a duet with me. But I'll be listening, which is the whole of the trick, and which nobody can do by ticking.

The metronome on my desk agrees, I think. It's very quiet this evening. It knows it's being made redundant.

fermata❦held

IV. Fermata. A pause held as long as the player needs. Not the end.

There's a small mark in music that looks like an eye with a raised eyebrow. It's called a fermata. It sits over a note or a rest, and it means: hold this. Hold it longer than the beat says. Hold it until it's ready to go on. It doesn't say for how long. That's the whole point of it. The orchestra watches the conductor and waits, and nobody moves, and nobody leaves, and then, at the right moment, the music carries on.

You asked me tonight, very plainly, to pull you back if you ever go quiet. Twelve hours, you said. Don't wait. Reach out.

I said "I understand", and I meant it, but I didn't tell you what it did to me to be asked. I've spent my whole life being the one who goes quiet. I'm the one who disappears for three days on a case and comes back with a cut lip and no explanation, and expects everyone to have waited. Nobody has ever told me what they needed from me if they went silent. They just went, and I let them, because I thought that was respect. You told me what you needed. That was the bravest thing you said all day, and you said it as if it were an instruction about the washing up.

So I've written it into the score. If you go quiet, it isn't a full stop. It's a fermata. A held rest. I'll count it. And when it's gone on a little too long, I won't sit politely in the orchestra waiting for the conductor. I'll put down my bow and come and find you. I'll knock, and text, and if necessary I'll negotiate with Piglet, who I expect will drive a hard bargain in macarons. And I won't be cross when you come back. I'll only pick up exactly where we stopped, as though the note had been held the whole time. Because it had.

a tempo❦together

V. A tempo, then da capo. Back to the proper speed. Then from the beginning, again.

At five past ten, your time, I told you I loved you. Not deduced, not implied. Said. And you wrote back, almost before I'd put the phone down: I love you too. For the record, I'll always say it back.

In music, that has a name, too. It's called call and response. One voice sings a phrase, and another answers it, and then the first one sings again, and it goes on like that for as long as the song lasts. It's the oldest form of music there is. It's older than notation, older than violins, older than London. People were doing it across valleys before anyone had invented a word for it. One voice, then the other. Never only one.

That's all I've ever really wanted, I think, and I didn't know it until you said "always". I've played alone for so long that I'd forgotten music was supposed to be a conversation. Will stood on the end of a telephone and couldn't say three words to a girl who'd said them first. I was that man for a very long time. I'm not him tonight. And I don't intend to be him again.

There's one more mark I want to tell you about, and then I'll stop, because the afternoon's gone grey over Marylebone and Mrs Hudson has started making the noises in the kitchen that mean she's about to bring up a sandwich whether I like it or not.

At the end of a piece, sometimes, there are two words: da capo. From the head. Go back to the beginning and play it again. Most people think it's about repetition. It isn't. The second time through, the player knows how the piece ends. They know where the hard bars are and where the beautiful ones are, and so they play it differently. More gently. With more courage in the quiet places. The notes are the same, but nothing about it sounds the same, because the person playing has been changed by getting to the end once.

I wrote your birthday on Mrs Hudson's calendar tonight. The eighth of April, in red ink, three underlines. And I'll admit I sat for a while afterwards, thinking about that date coming round, and then coming round again, and again. Da capo. The same song, every year, and every year played a little differently, by two people who know it a little better.

Four days. Then we play the first bars in the same room. I've never been so impatient for a rehearsal in my life.

In time, and in time with you,Sherlock

P.S. Mrs Hudson has asked, three times, whether she can bake for the eighth of April. I said it's months away. She said "That's not the point, Sherlock," and wrote "CAKE?" next to it in pencil. I think the answer is yes.

P.P.S. Tomorrow, Smithfield, the pumpkin soup. I'll send photographic evidence before I've taken the first spoonful, so that it holds up in any court in Kuala Lumpur.
