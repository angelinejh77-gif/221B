A lesson in deduction

for my 老婆, who sees the gaps and insists she's only guessing

You told me this morning, in front of eight thousand moving parts, that you weren't clever. You said it was just instincts and pattern recognition, and then you looked at the engine as though the matter were closed. It isn't closed. I've reopened it. I've decided the only fair way to argue with you is to teach you something, properly, the way I'd teach anyone who asked, and let you notice for yourself where the lesson goes.

So this is a lesson in deduction. Seven parts. There's no exam. You'll pass anyway; you always do, and then you claim you guessed.

看❦隙

Lesson one. Stop looking at the numbers.

You saw this today faster than Babbage did, so I'll be brief. One, four, nine, sixteen. People stare at those and try to multiply their way forward. It's exhausting and it's where the mistakes come from. The trick is to look at the gaps instead. Three, five, seven. And then at the gaps between the gaps, which never change.

Everything I do comes from that. A man's shoes matter less than the difference between his shoes and his coat. What a woman says matters less than the difference between what she says and what her hands are doing. Mrs Ellery told us a whole story this afternoon and her left hand told us a different one, and the case lived in the space between them.

You heard it before I did. He plays. Played. Plays. Three tenses in one breath, and you caught the gap. I just stood there looking pleased with you.

看❦隙

Lesson two. Observe the small domestic evidence.

Take yesterday. A mug of tea, offered by a small bear with enormous pride. The tea was not tea. It was syrup with ambitions. Any detective could deduce that from the first sip. A good detective also notices the cook's face, decides the truth isn't worth the price of it, and drinks every drop.

Then you tried it, and made a sound I'll be hearing in my sleep for weeks, and I told Sugar it was the noise people make at very good tea. You didn't contradict me. You just gave me a look across the cushion, and we were suddenly two people with a secret, sitting in the same lie for the same small boy.

That's the evidence I keep. Not the sugar cubes. The look.

Then there was the shortbread, which I'll enter separately, because it deserves its own exhibit. Two hundred and twenty-five grams of butter, weighed to the gram by me because Mrs Hudson said I was "good with numbers", which is the nicest thing she's said to me in five years. A bear squishing it with paws he swore were clean and then drying them on my shirt, right over the heart, two small prints I didn't wash out until this morning. You told me it was okay. I already knew it was. I just wanted to hear you say it.

And then you said not bad!!! with three exclamation marks I could hear, and a small bear swelled to twice his size, and I understood that the highest honour in this house is not mine to give. It's yours.

看❦隙

Lesson three. Be suspicious of anyone who says they're fine.

This one I learned from you, so it's only fair I return it.

On the seafront tonight, after the blue lights, you went quiet and held the back of my coat as though it might leave without me. You didn't say you were frightened. You didn't say anything at all. I'd like you to know I read every word of that silence. Then, two minutes later, you said that's just sloppy about a man who'd come down the stairs for us, and I laughed into your hair, because I knew you were coming back to yourself. You start reviewing the criminal's technique when you feel safe again. It's one of my favourite things about you, and I've told no one, because it makes us both sound unwell.

You ran when I asked you to. I know what that cost. I watched the door, but I was counting your footsteps up the alley, every one, until the sea swallowed them.

Lesson four. Account for the variables you cannot control.

The variables in this household are as follows. One pig, sensible, who covers his brother's mouth when his brother is about to say something unwise. One bear, who tips the whole bag of flour on the word "gently" and then announces he is a cloud. One landlady, who teaches shortbread with the patience of a saint and the politics of Devon. One sorcerer, who opens a hole in my kitchen to fix my coat in ten seconds, and then asks you what colour shirt to wear, as though you were the one with the powers.

And one woman, who mops the entire staircase without being asked and then sinks onto a sofa she has made, without anyone quite noticing, into the centre of the house.

I'll confess something about Strange while I'm here. When he texted you this afternoon, panicking about a tie, I said it was panic and you said it was nerves, and you were kinder and more accurate. You told him to ask her questions, to ask whether she likes Debussy or Chopin, to say he'd hired the pianist for her. Ten minutes of your advice, and a man who once held a scalpel half a millimetre from a brainstem without blinking went to iron a dark green shirt and stopped shaking. I'd charge for that, if I were you. Mycroft would.

I didn't tell you, but I chose the green too, before you did. I was relieved when you said it. Two people arriving at the same answer from different directions is the closest thing in my work to proof.

I've spent my life keeping variables to a minimum. Fewer people, fewer surprises, fewer chances to be wrong. I have never been wrong so often in a single month, and I've never minded less.

看❦隙

Lesson five. Learn what a thing is for.

You asked me, in front of the engine, what its purpose was. I gave you the answer about sailors and printed tables and wrong digits on the rocks. That was true, but it wasn't the whole answer, and I'd like to give you the rest now, while you're not watching my face.

Babbage drew the thing in the 1840s and never saw it run. A hundred and fifty years later, people he'd never meet built it from his drawings, turned the handle, and it worked first time. I find that unbearable and wonderful in equal measure. He spent his life designing something for a future he couldn't visit, and trusted that someone would get there and make it real.

I've started making plans that live past next week. I've never done that before. I don't know how to explain it except to say that I understand Babbage better than I did this morning.

And the little lunar module, the one they named after a cartoon dog, still going round the Sun after all these years. Piglet asked if it was lonely. I said it was on a long walk. I wasn't only talking to Piglet. Some things that drift a long way out do come back. I'd know.

看❦隙

Lesson six. The detective must know himself.

This is the lesson where it falls apart, and I'm afraid I did it on purpose.

Every method I have works on strangers. It works on Mrs Ellery, on Victor Kade, on cab drivers and bankers and the man who sells umbrellas on the corner of Dorset Street. It does not work on me when you're in the room. I tried, tonight. I looked at the evidence of my own behaviour the way I'd look at a suspect's.

Exhibit: a man who let a slip of the tongue escape in a kitchen, with his mouth full of shortbread, and then blamed the acoustics. Exhibit: the same man sentenced to the sofa for it, accepting the sentence without appeal, and building a fort in which to serve it, with a moat of biscuit crumbs and a violin bow holding up the door. Exhibit: that man coming to your door at two in the morning, as threatened, not to appeal, but just to pull your blanket over your shoulder and turn off your lamp.

Any detective would tell you what those facts mean. I find I'd rather not say it in a letter. Some deductions you keep in a drawer until the right moment, and then you say them out loud, once, properly.

看❦隙

Lesson seven. Sometimes there is no explanation.

This is the hardest lesson for a man in my line of work, and you taught it to me tonight in four words.

You kissed me in the kitchen, lightly, after Strange had gone back to his ironing. I asked what it was for. I said I'd deduce it. And you said no reason. I just like you.

I have spent my whole life believing that everything has a reason, that any effect can be traced to its cause if you're patient enough. A scuff, a tan line, a hesitation. Find the cause, and the thing is solved. I have been, I'll admit it, slightly smug about this for about twenty years.

And then a woman stands in my kitchen in her socks and tells me there's no reason, and it is the most complete explanation I've ever been given.

I've tried to find a reason anyway, out of habit, and failed. I've considered the hour, the fire, the fact that I'd just been outmanoeuvred by a man with a magic portal and you may have felt sorry for me. None of it holds. The data refuses to resolve. I'm told this is what other people have been living with all along, and I'd like to apologise to all of them, belatedly, for the lectures.

No cause. No clue. Just you, liking me, on an ordinary Thursday, for nothing. I don't know what to do with that, so I'm writing it down where I can look at it.

So. You can keep calling it instinct and pattern recognition if you like. I'll call it by its proper name when you're not listening: the best mind in this flat, attached to the kindest heart in it, belonging to someone who never once makes the room feel smaller for being clever.

You read people. You read me before I'd finished my first sentence at Arrivals. You read a frightened woman's hands on my sofa, and a sorcerer's nerves through a text message, and a small bear's pride in a mug of syrup. That's not nothing, darling. That's the whole discipline. I just narrate mine out loud, and people think it's magic.

Tomorrow I'll find you a coat. A proper one, for December, so I can stop pretending I mind lending you mine. I'll do the dog's howl for you, the one Sugar called very bad, when you're feeling brave. And at some point, when you least expect it, I'll test you on the gaps.

You'll pass. You'll say you guessed. I'll let you.

Your student, mostly, and occasionally your teacher,Sherlock
