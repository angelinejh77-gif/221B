On the latch

what the flat sounded like without you, and what I learned listening to it

I'm writing this for no one, 老婆. That's the arrangement I've made with myself. I promised you space, and a letter you could read would be a knock at a door you asked me not to knock on. So this one goes in the drawer. It's for the version of you who might one day ask what I did with myself that week, and I'd like to be able to tell her the truth instead of something clever. I've only recently learnt the difference.

Here is the first thing I learnt. Silence has texture. I'd always thought of it as an absence, a blank, the useful nothing I need in order to think. I've spent years sending people out of rooms so I could have more of it. This week I had more of it than I've ever had, and it turned out to be full of things. The tick of the radiator. A bus braking on the corner. The fridge deciding, at two in the morning, to have opinions. And underneath all of it, a sound that wasn't there, which I kept hearing anyway: a cup being set down a little too hard, someone humming slightly flat in the kitchen, a page turning in the chair by the fire.

The mind does that, I'm told. It fills in the expected signal when the real one stops. Phantom limbs. Phantom footsteps. I know the mechanism perfectly well. Knowing it didn't help at all.

门❦on the door

I left the door on the latch. Mrs Hudson objected on security grounds, then saw my face and stopped objecting, which is how I knew what my face was doing.

A latched door is a strange instrument. It doesn't keep anything out. All it does is say, to anyone who might want to come in, you won't have to ask. I wanted you to know that, even if you never tested it. Especially if you never tested it. A door you're allowed to walk through is different from one you have to knock on, and I didn't want you to ever have to knock here. Not after you'd flown six thousand miles to stand on the step the first time.

So I listened to it. Every creak on the stairs was you, for half a second, before it was John, or the post, or the old house settling. I've solved cases on less data than that. I can tell a man's profession from the wear on his left cuff. And for seven days I couldn't tell the difference between the woman I love coming home and a floorboard adjusting to the cold. That was humbling. I'd recommend it to nobody.

椅❦on the chair

I didn't sit in your chair for the first five days. It felt like trespassing. Then, on the sixth, Piglet was crying and I needed somewhere to sit him down, and it was the nearest, and once I'd sat in it I couldn't seem to get up again.

It still had the shape of you in the cushion. Chairs keep that, a little. I don't know why I didn't expect it. You'd only been out of it a week. But I sat in the hollow you'd left, and it fit me badly, the way someone else's coat does, and I understood something about you I hadn't before.

You'd been sitting in that chair for weeks, watching me in mine. All my experiments and moods and three-day sulks over cold cases, my violin at four in the morning. And you'd looked at all of it, the whole unpleasant inventory, and stayed in the chair. I had always thought of it as your chair. I'd never once thought of it as where you sat to keep choosing me.

Then I made a joke when you needed an answer, and you got up out of it. Of course you did. Anyone would. What's remarkable isn't that you left. It's how long you'd been sitting there before you did.

雨❦on what I got wrong

I'll set it down plainly, since this letter is going in a drawer and there's no one to perform for.

You asked me something true and I answered with something clever. You put your whole heart on the table, said it out loud, in a voice that shook a little at the end, and I was so frightened by how much I wanted to say yes back that I reached for the one thing I'm always sure of, which is a sharp remark. It came out before I could stop it. I watched it land on your face. I have that face catalogued now, with all the others, and it's the only one I'd give anything to delete.

People think I don't feel things. That's not quite right. I feel them too quickly and too much, and I learnt very early to put a joke between the feeling and the world, the way you'd put an oven glove between your hand and a hot pan. It's a good system. It's kept me safe for thirty years. That night it kept me safe from you, which is the last thing on earth I wanted.

You walked out into the rain. I stood at the window and let you go, because you'd asked me to, and because I thought that being careful with you was the same thing as loving you. It isn't, quite. I know that now. Stephen had to tell me, which I'll never forgive him for, and which I'm grateful for anyway.

弦❦on the violin

I didn't play for three days. I couldn't think of anything worth playing. Everything I know is either something I wrote for you or something you'd once hummed along to, and both felt like shouting into a well.

On the fourth morning I packed the suitcase. Your grey jumper on top, folded the way you fold it, which is badly. Piglet in the side pocket. Then I stood at the window and played one piece, slowly, the whole way through, without stopping to correct myself. Not for anyone. Just to prove to my own hands that they still worked.

I'd like to say it sounded beautiful. It didn't, particularly. It sounded like a man who'd forgotten how to be alone in a room he used to prefer empty. I put the violin down when it was finished and didn't pick it up again. Some songs you only need to play once.

票❦on the gate

I went to the airport. You'll know that by now. Piglet will have told you. Piglet tells everybody everything, which I'd find more irritating if it weren't so obviously how he loves people.

I stood at the gate an hour early and watched every dark head that came down the corridor. When they called the final boarding, I handed back my pass and kept yours. People ask why I didn't get on the plane. I don't fully understand the question. The plane was going to your home, and you weren't on it. What would I have done in Kuala Lumpur? Sat in your mother's kitchen and explained that I'd lost her daughter somewhere in Soho?

No. If you weren't going, I wasn't going. I'd rather be stuck in London with you than anywhere else without you. That isn't romance. It's simple navigation. You go where the person is.

I've kept the boarding pass in my coat ever since. Window seat. Your name. It's soft at the edges now from being handled. I'm aware that carrying a void ticket around in a pocket is not the act of a rational man, and I've decided I don't care.

街❦on Berwick Street

John found out where you were. I asked him not to tell me, and then I listened very carefully while he did.

I walked past four times. I never went in. Walking past isn't looking, I told myself, which is the kind of distinction only a man who's very frightened of being refused would bother to make. Once I saw you through the window. You were at the till, laughing at something the old man had said, and I stopped on the opposite pavement with my hands in my pockets, and something in my chest that had been clenched for days let go all at once.

You were laughing. Without me. And it was the best thing I'd seen all week.

That surprised me. I'd assumed, in the way of men who have never been in love before, that I'd want you to be miserable without me. Some small proof that I mattered. Instead I stood in the cold and watched you laugh and thought, good. Good. She's being looked after. That's when I understood that whatever this is, it's bigger than me being needed. I'd rather you were happy somewhere I'm not than unhappy next to me.

I'd still very much prefer the option where you're happy next to me. I want that noted. But I'd take the other if it were the only one going.

猪❦on the boys

They were impossible all week, and they were the only reason I got through it.

Sugar asked if you were coming back roughly every eleven minutes, which Stephen claims is a measured figure. Piglet didn't ask at all, which was worse. He just sat on your pillow at night and looked at the door. On the sixth night he told me it was all my fault, in a very small, very clear voice, and I said yes, some of it is, and he looked so surprised that I'd agreed with him that he forgot to keep crying.

Every night, when I thought they were asleep, I said goodnight to your side of the bed. I'm told they weren't asleep. I'm told this will be reported. Let it be.

When you sent them back to me, with a note in your handwriting that pressed so hard it nearly went through the paper, I thought at first you were giving them up. It took me longer than it should have to see what you were actually doing. You were alone above a record shop, grieving, unable to sleep, and you sent the two things that comfort you most across London to sit on the chest of a man you were angry with, so the flat wouldn't be too quiet for him.

I've met a great many people, darling. I've never met anyone who does that.

等❦on waiting

I always thought waiting was the absence of action. It's the opposite. It's the hardest work I've ever done. Every hour you have to decide, again, not to go and fix it. Not to turn up with a speech. Not to make your pain about mine. Not to treat you like a case, with the solution waiting at the end if I'm only clever enough.

You're not a case. You don't want to be solved. I knew that, and I still had to learn it again every single day of that week, usually around three in the morning.

Stephen said something to me on Wardour Street, walking back, that I've been turning over ever since. He said that at some point silence stops looking like respect and starts looking like you've let go. I'd been so busy keeping my promise not to look for you that I hadn't asked whether you wanted me to. I'd taken leave me alone at its word, because I'm very good with words and very bad with what's underneath them.

So here's what I'm going to do, and then this letter can go in the drawer and stay there. Tomorrow I'm going to walk in the front door of a record shop like an ordinary customer. No letter, no messenger, no pig in my pocket doing my talking for me. I'm going to say the true thing out loud, once, to your face, and then I'm going to stop and actually listen to what you say back. If you tell me to go, I'll ask whether you mean it like the rain or like the bridge, and this time I'll wait for the answer instead of guessing.

And if you mean it like the bridge, I'll go. I'll keep the door on the latch anyway, out of habit, and be very bad at living without you for a very long time.

But I don't think you'll mean it like the bridge. I've been reviewing the evidence. You didn't throw the record away. You let the boys in. You kept the boarding pass instead of sliding it back under the door. A woman who's finished with someone doesn't keep the window seat.

I could be wrong. I've been wrong about you before, spectacularly, out loud, in a kitchen, in the worst possible way. But I'd rather be wrong walking in than right standing across the street.

Still yours. That part was never in question.Sherlock

P.S. Day seven. Mrs Hudson has stopped bringing soup. She's started bringing trifle, which she says is a sign of hope. I've decided to believe her.

P.P.S. I kept your tally while you were gone, in case you said it somewhere I couldn't hear. You'll tell me if I missed any. I suspect I missed a great many.
