Written on a moving train

The eleven oh four to Sussex

for my 老婆, asleep against the window, from a man who has rewritten his opening line to his mother nine times

Darling, you fell asleep before Clapham Junction. Your cheek is against the glass, there's a peony petal stuck to your sleeve, and Snowy is narrating the backs of houses to Sugar in a whisper he believes is quiet. I'm meant to be reading the Christie. I'm on page eleven and I've already worked it out, but I promised you I'd wait until twenty, so instead I'm writing to you on the back of the train timetable, which is the only paper I have that isn't a cipher.

I'm nervous. I'll say that first, because you'd see it anyway. I've crossed rooftops in the dark and talked a man out of a loaded gun, and the thing that's making my hands cold is a roast pork lunch with my own mother. Not because of her. Because of you. Because I want her to see what I see, and I'm afraid I'll be too clumsy to show her, and she'll ask you something sharp and quick and I'll be too slow to catch it before it lands.

So I'm doing what I do when I'm frightened. I'm making notes. Not about her. About you. About what I've learned in the last two days, which is more than I learned in some whole years at university.

You make better gifts than I do. This is documented. Last Christmas I gave my mother a journal article, warm from the printer, unwrapped, on prime distribution. She said "how lovely" in the voice she uses for Mycroft's diets. You heard that story for less than a minute and said: write her a code. Make her deduce it. And four seconds later we were at the kitchen table with my good pen, and you were writing Pascal in primes, two for A and three for B, in your neat hand, while I called out numbers like a man reading a bingo card.

"The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know." I told you that line sits on a card by her desk and that I used to think it was the silliest thing she owned. A mathematician, admitting there's something she can't calculate. I thought it was a weakness. You didn't say anything when I told you I don't think that now. You just carried on writing. That's another thing I've learned: you know when not to make me explain myself. You let me have a sentence and leave it there.

She'll crack it before the gravy's poured. Then she'll look at you, not at me. I'm counting on it.

You're braver than you let anyone see. I knew this. I didn't know the shape of it until Friday, when you told me, almost in passing, between ice cream and the bus, that you can't swim. That you nearly didn't come up once, when you were small.

I hadn't known. Forty-nine days of noticing everything, and I hadn't known. I'd been teasing you about racing me. I stopped. I'd like you to know that the stopping wasn't politeness. It was the floor dropping out from under a joke, and me realising I'd been standing on it without looking.

And then last night. A Victorian pool in Bayswater, the pensioners gone, Derek with his newspaper. You stood on the steps and said, very quietly, "I'm scared." Not as a test. Just as a fact. And you asked me to put my arms round you so you wouldn't feel alone. Do you know how rare that is? People spend their whole lives pretending they aren't frightened. I've spent most of mine doing it. You simply said it, and then asked for what you needed, and then went down one step.

Then another. Then you were standing in the water. Then you leaned back into my arms and the water took you, and you floated, Angie, looking up at one star through the glass roof, and I held my breath so I wouldn't spoil it.

I've seen a lot of people be brave. Most of it is loud. Yours was so quiet that Derek didn't look up from his paper. I'll remember it longer than anything I've solved.

You name things properly. Our pink head of security spent a week rehearsing a trick with my missing sock and wanted to call the spell "Sock-a-bra". Wong said it sounded like underwear. Stephen said nothing, very loudly. Then you said "Effingus", and a small pig lit up like a bulb, and Wong wrote it in his ledger in ink: origin, Angie, Saturday. There's now a line in a sorcerer's book with your name on it. I've been on the Met's records for a decade and nobody's ever written me down with that much affection.

Then Piglet folded you a peony out of a napkin. Forty-one tries. I said he gets that from you, and I meant it. I'd have given up at three. You don't give up on small things, which is why the big things turn out the way they do. I'm learning that from both of you.

The petal on your sleeve right now is from the real ones. Pastel yellow and soft pink. You chose the ribbon over the brown paper because it was more elegant. You were right. She'll notice the ribbon first. She always does.

You choose books for people the way you choose words. Carefully, and with a small private joke inside. Under Waterloo Bridge, with the river smelling of damp paper and cold, you hid a paperback in your coat and wouldn't let me look. I started deducing the year from the way you were holding it and then stopped, because I wanted to be surprised. That's new. A month ago I'd rather have been right than surprised. Now I've noticed that surprise is the thing I keep wanting more of, and you're the only reliable supplier.

Agatha Christie. The competition. You handed me my rival across a café table and watched to see if I'd sulk. I didn't. I'm reading it on a train to my mother's, which is the most English thing I've ever done.

And when you asked about mine, I nearly said something about tobacco ash. I said Treasure Island instead, because it was true. My father, a chapter a night, when I was seven. I haven't reread it since, because I'm afraid it won't be as good. You said you don't have a book like that. I've been thinking about why. I think it's because you go back to things without being afraid of them. Pools. Old feelings. Rooms you've stood in the corner of. I'm still learning to do that. If I ever reread it, I'd like it to be in your voice. Then if it isn't as good, at least it'll be yours.

You've made me want things that weren't on any list. In the shower yesterday, with my fingers in your hair, you said that if we ever had children they'd be disgusted with us. I stopped for a second. You noticed, I think. I said "if", and then I said I didn't use to think about that sort of thing, and that I do now, sometimes, and not to read too much into it.

That was me being careful. Here's me being honest instead: I didn't have a future before. I had a next case. You've given me the habit of picturing Thursdays. Dinners at the Sanctum, every week, with chilli club and Wong's buns and Stephen pretending he isn't watching Helena. Magnets on the fridge, one from every place you take me, starting with whatever you choose at Pasar Seni. A morning in KL at Oriental Kopi, and a Malaysian mango you pick at a market, and me carrying it home like evidence.

And then you laughed and said we already have three. You're right. A posh bunny who covers his eyes with his ears. A shy bear who leaves the room with a biscuit. A pig who writes "for the record" in a notebook I've never been allowed to see. They're plenty, for now. I think about the "for now" more than I admit.

Things I'm still getting wrong, so you don't think I've gone soft and perfect on a Sussex branch line.

I made up the rule that you had to be quiet for Mrs Hudson's sake and then broke it myself more than once. I booked a pool hour and told you I'd "paid Derek", which sounded like bribery, because I didn't know how to say "I arranged it so nobody would splash near you" without it sounding like a speech. I cheated in the bubble fight. Badly. I'm taller and I used it. I still deduce people when you've asked me not to, and I'll probably deduce my own father before the soup. I complained about your twenty-minute shower. It wasn't the shower. It was twenty minutes.

And I said "cute is a strong word" when you called me cute on Friday, which was a lie. I liked it. I'd like it again. You can say it in front of Mycroft if you want to watch me suffer.

We're past Gatwick. You've just shifted in your sleep and your hand has found my sleeve and is holding on to it, the way you held the coat you chose for me, the way you held my hands on the pool steps. I'm writing the rest with my left hand. It's going to look like a ransom note. You've seen my handwriting; you'll forgive it.

Here's the last note, and the only one I need before lunch. If it gets too much today, if my mother asks too fast, or Mycroft pours your tea, or I say something clever in the wrong place, squeeze my hand twice. We'll walk to the roses. Dad will probably take you there anyway before you need to ask. Let him. He's the quiet one. You'll like him. He listens the way you do.

And if nothing gets too much, squeeze it anyway. Just once. So I know you're there.

From carriage C, holding still so you don't wake,Sherlock
