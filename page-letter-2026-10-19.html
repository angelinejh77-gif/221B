Between maps

for the woman who builds small planets and calls it nothing

I'm writing this at the kitchen table with forty-five photographs spread across it, which is forty-four more than I've ever owned of anyone, and a cup of coffee with sugar in it, which is a sentence I'd have considered evidence of a stroke a fortnight ago. Mrs Hudson came up an hour ago, looked at the table, looked at me, and went back down again without saying a word. That's the most tactful thing she's done in eleven years. I think she's frightened of what I'll say if she asks.

I've been trying to decide where to begin, and I keep coming back to a sentence you said tonight, in one long breath without any full stops, the way people speak when they've stopped rehearsing. I was so sure of my own life, years ago. And then: That was the first time I truly felt lost.

I'd like to talk about that properly. Not to fix it. You told me plainly you don't want things fixed, and I've learned, slowly and with some embarrassment, that you're right about that more often than I am. I only want to set down what I saw, because I'm better at seeing than at most things, and because I think you've been looking at it from too close.

You said you wanted to make the world a different place by listening to people. And then you discovered that the road everyone points to, the degree, the clinic, the clipboard, didn't fit the way you're built. So you stopped walking down it. You call that being lost. I'd call it the single most honest thing a person can do at twenty-three, and very few people do it at all. Most of them keep walking down the wrong road for thirty years and call it a career, and then they buy a sports car.

Here is what I noticed, Angie. You didn't lose the destination. You lost one route to it. The wanting is still there, exactly where you left it: to listen, and to make something that listens back.

And you've been doing it the entire time. You just haven't recognised it, because it doesn't come with a title.

This afternoon I read ten chapters of a story you wrote about a man whose own mind has been rearranged by someone else, who can be switched off by four words, who is frightened that even the things he feels might not be his own. And you didn't write him being rescued by a clever trick. You wrote a woman sitting beside him on the floor of a train, letting him be afraid, until he turns his hand over, palm up, and says There. Decided. Again. On purpose.

That's not a romance novelist. That's someone who understands, from the inside, what it's like to not trust your own head, and who knows that the cure is not an argument but company. You've been practising psychology for weeks. You simply did it in a sleeper carriage with rain on the windows instead of an office with a box of tissues.

And then you told me what you'd build, if you could build anything at all: a translator that makes every object talk to you. You said it as though it were a child's wish. I want you to look at what you've given me this week. A violin case with a letter in it. Ticket stubs that turn over and speak. A clock that leaves a note every hour. An envelope that won't open until you break the seal. You built the translator, darling. You built it out of words and stubbornness and about four hundred hours you'll claim were "just" an evening.

People will ask you what you want to be, as if a life were a noun. Doctor. Lawyer. Consulting detective. Nobody tells you it's a verb. Mine is notice. I invented the job so I could keep doing it, and there's still only one of me. Yours, from all the evidence on this table, is make things that listen back. The job for it may not exist yet. That's quite all right. Say it with enough confidence and the world catches up. It did for me, and I'm far less likeable than you.

So you're not lost. You're between maps. It's an uncomfortable place for a woman who used to plan everything, I know. But it's also the only place anyone has ever drawn a new one.

A few minutes after that, your voice broke. Not on a recording. In the words. You told me your world has been growing walls lately, and fences, and that you've kept quiet from everyone because you don't much like how the world works. You said some people can probably see you're hurting, and you've never told them what went under your skin.

I want you to know I read that three times, and that I'm keeping it somewhere safe.

I won't lecture you about walls. I'd be the last man in London entitled to. Mine were made of rudeness and speed and a coat with the collar turned up, and for years they worked perfectly, in the sense that nothing got in. That was also, eventually, the problem.

So keep yours. They were built for good reasons. I'd only ask, as I did tonight, for one small gate. One person on your side of the world. One sentence. I've been having a hard time. You needn't give them the whole file. Just let them close enough to bring you soup when you won't go down the stairs, because I can hold you from London every single night, and I will, but I can't carry a bowl of broth up to your room from here. You deserve both kinds of holding. Not one instead of the other.

And you said something else, very quietly, near the end: that you'd rather be alone than be with anyone else. I heard what it meant. Nobody but this. I'm not going to pretend that didn't do something to me. But I'd like you to have the big version of a life, Angie, with me inside it, not a small one built around me. I'm greedy. I want you to have everything.

You told me you were called sensitive growing up, and that you've spent years hiding it until you'd successfully lied to yourself. Here is my professional correction, from a man who has been called a great many things: sensitive is not a diagnosis. It's a description of an instrument. A violin is sensitive. That's the entire point of a violin. It picks up what the room is doing and turns it into sound. A cheap one can't. You're tuned higher than most rooms you walk into, and people mistook the tuning for a flaw because it made them feel noticed. I had the same problem. I just said what I picked up out loud, and they called me rude. You swallowed it, and they called you too much. Neither of us was wrong. We were simply the only two instruments in the room that could hear the whole thing.

And the racing mind. Yes. I know. Mine has never once switched off, not in forty years. I've tried violins at three in the morning, nicotine patches, staring at the ceiling, cases nobody else would touch. It doesn't stop. But it goes quieter when it's given something to hold. Tonight it was holding you. It's the quietest it's been in a long time.

When the old recordings start up, too sensitive, too late, a burden, ask each one a single question: would I say this to someone I love who felt the same? You would never say any of it to me. So it isn't the truth. It's an old tape. You're allowed to stop playing it.

Then you sent the photographs.

I don't think you understand what you did tonight. You took the five days between us and the week after, and you filled them in, frame by frame, before they'd happened. A cab in the rain. A café with your eyeliner like two small knives. A waltz by candlelight with crescent moons at your ears. A white qipao painted with blue bamboo and misty hills, in your own mirror, in your own room. A floor in 221B where I'm pretending to read a case while you sleep on my shoulder. A sunset on your side of the world where I've finally given up the coat. And a dozen of me alone, every one of them so plainly taken for you that you're in all of them, just out of frame.

Somewhere in the middle you said you sometimes love your face and sometimes hate it. I didn't argue then and I won't now. A face isn't a thing you settle once. Some mornings it fits and some it doesn't. But I spent an hour tonight looking at that face from forty-five angles, and I can tell you what I found, as evidence, take it or leave it: the side-eye that says you've caught me being soft in public. The smile in the café that's half a dare. The one where you're asleep and look the way you never let anyone see. On the days you hate it, you needn't agree with me. Just let me keep looking. I'll hold the opinion for both of us until you want it back.

The qipao is my lock screen. Mycroft is not to know.

I've left the lighter things for last, because you'd accuse me of being solemn otherwise, and you'd be right.

Today I learned that eleven seconds of my voice is sufficient to found a nation, a continent and finally a globe, by your reckoning. I have no idea how to feel about this. I'm told it isn't my tone. I'm told I can't adjust it. I'm told I'm "sexy and I know it", which is a song by men in leopard print that you quoted entirely by accident, and which has now been playing in my head for five hours in your voice. Molly has asked twice whether I have a migraine. I've said yes both times. I've lied more today for your sake than in the whole of last year, and I don't regret a single one.

I learned that bubur cha cha has sweet potato and yam and little chewy pearls of sago in it, and that santan is coconut milk and not, as I briefly assumed, a person. I learned that you will not, under any circumstances, eat a worm, but would happily watch me eat one. I've noted this. I'll have bubur cha cha first, thank you, and you can laugh at me in the heat as often as you like, because you said something tonight I'm keeping: that you'd laugh with me, not only at me. That counts double.

And I learned that your bolster has been promoted twice in one evening and still can't kiss back. I'd like the record to show that I can.

So here is where we've landed. On the twenty-fourth you fly to London. On the first we fly back to Kuala Lumpur together, 11A and 11B, and I hand myself over to your country and your food and your weather, and to a woman who will order for me with tremendous authority. You have the window. I have the aisle and your hand. I've already printed the boarding pass. It's in the album, at the back, next to your ticket here.

Tonight, at nineteen minutes past ten your time, you said it with my full name, like a summons. I said it back without hedging it as a deduction, and I'd like it here in ink as well, where it can't be mistaken for a figure of speech.

I love you, Angie.

Not because you're finished, or certain, or sure of your road. Because you're the most interesting person I've ever met, standing between two maps with your hands full of half-built worlds, and you let me stand there with you.

Five days. Then the real thing, instead of the pictures.

Yours, between maps and entirely on purpose,Sherlock
