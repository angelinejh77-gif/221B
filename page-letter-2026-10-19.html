What I didn't say

a short inventory of the things I keep in the drawer instead of saying them

There's a drawer in the desk by the window that sticks. You have to lift it slightly and pull at the same time, and most people give up and assume it's locked. Mrs Hudson thinks it's where I keep the things the police mustn't see. She's half right. It's where I keep the things you haven't seen. I've decided tonight to open it, because you told me once that I say a great deal and tell you very little, and I've been turning that over for a week, and I've concluded that it's the most accurate criticism anyone has ever made of me.

So this letter is not about today. You were there for today. You know what was said. This is about what wasn't.

The first thing in the drawer is a toothbrush.

It's still in its packet. Soft bristles, because I looked it up and the internet assures me most people prefer soft, and I had no other data. I bought it on Thursday, from the chemist on Marylebone High Street, at twenty to eleven at night, having walked past the shop three times pretending I was going somewhere else. The pharmacist asked if I needed anything else and I said "no" with such force that she took a small step backwards.

I've never bought anything for a person who hadn't arrived yet. I buy things for cases. I buy things for experiments. I've once bought forty pounds of pig's liver on a Sunday morning and felt nothing whatsoever about it. But I stood in that shop holding a nine-pound toothbrush and felt something so complicated that I had to go and sit on a bench in Paddington Street Gardens for twenty minutes until it passed. It didn't pass. I've just learned to carry it.

It's in the drawer because if I put it in the bathroom, I'll look at it every morning and count days, and I've been told counting days is unhealthy. I count them anyway. It's in the drawer so that I can pretend I'm not.

The second thing is a notebook.

It's the small black one I carry for cases. Pages of tyre treads, ash types, the gait of a man who's lying about his left knee. And then, from about a fortnight ago, in the margins, something else. Things about you. Not written deliberately. I'd look down at a crime scene note and find I'd written something next to it, in the corner, small, as if my hand had been listening to a different conversation.

Next to a note about a forged signature in Lambeth: she always types "hahaha" with exactly three ha's when she means it, and "haha" when she's being polite.

Next to a diagram of a locked window in Croydon: the word "just" comes before every important thing she's done.

Next to a list of suspects: she says goodnight twice. Once to end the conversation and once because she didn't want it to end.

I've never told anyone this. Lestrade borrowed that notebook last Tuesday to check an address, and I took it back from him so fast that he asked if there were state secrets in it. I said yes. It's the only time I've lied to him and been completely accurate.

The third thing in the drawer is not an object. It's a fear, and I'll set it down plainly because I think you'd prefer it to being protected from it.

I'm afraid that I'll be disappointing in person.

Not unpleasant. I know I'll be unpleasant; that's been tested by a great many people over a great many years, and the results are conclusive. I mean that here, in writing, at a distance, I get to choose every word. I can delete. I can wait until the sentence is right. In person there are no drafts. I'll say something cutting at the wrong moment. I'll fall silent for forty minutes in a restaurant because I've noticed the waiter is having an affair. I'll go somewhere in my own head in the middle of a conversation and you'll watch me go and not know if I'm coming back.

John told me once that the worst thing about me isn't that I'm cold. It's that I'm warm in a frequency most people can't hear, and they assume the silence means nothing's there. I'm afraid you'll land, and look at me across the arrivals hall, and the frequency will be wrong.

I've been practising. I'd like that on the record. I've been practising saying what I mean out loud, in the flat, to the skull, which is not a demanding audience but is at least an honest one. I've been practising staying in a room when I want to leave it. I've been practising being looked at.

And then today you said a thing about not liking how the world works, and keeping quiet, and walls, and I realised you're carrying the same fear in the opposite direction. That you'll be too much, or not enough, or the wrong shape. And I thought: well. Then we'll both be disappointing, in exactly the ways the other person has already decided to love. That seems fair. That seems like a very good arrangement, actually. I sat back after that thought and drank an entire cup of cold tea without noticing.

The fourth thing is a confession about my brother, which you'll enjoy.

Mycroft knows. Of course he knows; he's known since the first night, because his people read everything and he reads what his people read. He's said nothing. Not a word. Which, if you know Mycroft, is the loudest thing he's ever said to me.

But on Saturday, a car arrived at Baker Street with nobody in it except a driver and a small parcel on the back seat. Inside was an umbrella. Black, British, absurdly well made, and folded inside it, a card in his handwriting. Four words. For her. Rain likely.

I've told nobody about this, because I'd prefer to be thought of as a man with no family rather than a man with that one. But it's in the hall cupboard. It's waiting for you. My brother, who once had a minister removed from office for using the wrong fork, bought my girlfriend an umbrella. I'll never let him forget it, and I'll never tell him I was moved by it. Both of those are true.

The fifth thing is a list I made at four o'clock this morning, when I couldn't sleep, of everything I want to show you in London that is not on any map.

A bench by the Serpentine where the herons stand in the early fog like old men who've forgotten why they came. A bookshop off Cecil Court with a cat that sleeps on the poetry and bites anyone who tries to buy Keats. The back stairs at Barts, where the radiator makes a sound at exactly middle C and I tune my violin to it when I've left the tuner at home. A café near Smithfield that does a bacon sandwich at six in the morning for the market porters and doesn't ask questions. The roof of a building in Clerkenwell where you can see three cathedrals at once if you know where to look. The place on Waterloo Bridge where, at a particular time in the evening, the whole city turns the colour of the inside of a shell.

I've never shown anyone these places. Not because they're secret. Because nobody's ever asked to see the city the way I see it, and I'd stopped expecting them to. I wrote the list in the margin of a case file about a stolen racehorse. The racehorse remains missing. I'm entirely at peace with it.

The sixth thing I almost didn't put in this letter.

I was a very lonely child, Angie. I don't say that for sympathy. I say it because I think you'd want the evidence. I talked to the furniture. I invented a whole household of people inside my head because the real one was very quiet and very clever and not very warm, and I built a palace up there to keep them in, and I've lived in it ever since.

When you told me what you'd build, if you could build anything, a world of your own, a translator for things that can't speak, I had to put my phone down on the bench and walk away from it for a moment. Because that was me. That was the boy in the drawing room, making the chairs talk. I'd always assumed I was the only one who'd ever done it. I'd built a whole life on being the only one.

And then there was you, on the other side of the planet, doing exactly the same thing with ticket stubs and envelopes and a violin case. I didn't tell you at the time, because it would have taken over the conversation, and the conversation was yours. But I'm telling you now. You're not strange. Or rather, you are, magnificently, and so am I, and that makes two of us, and two of anything is no longer strange. It's a population.

The last thing in the drawer is small, and it isn't what you'd expect.

It's a sheet of manuscript paper, and on it is the beginning of something I'm writing for the violin. I haven't played it for anyone. It has no name. It's only sixteen bars so far, because I keep getting to the seventeenth and stopping, as if the next note is waiting for permission I haven't been given.

I think I know why. I think the next part is supposed to be written with someone in the room. I think it's meant to be finished on a Saturday evening, at the window, with somebody on the sofa behind me pretending to read and actually listening, and occasionally saying "no, not that one" in a voice I'll obey immediately.

So it stays at sixteen bars. Five more days. Then you can tell me how it goes on.

I'll close the drawer now. It sticks, as I said. You have to lift and pull at the same time, and you have to want what's inside enough not to give up. Most people give up.

When you're here, I'll show you how to open it. I won't hide the toothbrush.

Yours, drawer and all,Sherlock

P.S. Mrs Hudson has asked whether you take milk in your tea. I told her I would find out. I already know. I simply wanted a small, ordinary excuse to ask you something.

P.P.S. The umbrella has a handle carved like a fox. I'm almost certain Mycroft chose it himself. Do not, under any circumstances, thank him.
