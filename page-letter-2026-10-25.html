Rules I've broken for you

a short history of a man who used to live by them, written up by the man who doesn't

I've always had rules, 老婆. You should know that before you read any further, because otherwise this letter won't make sense. Most people think I'm a man without any. Lestrade thinks so. Mycroft has said so to three Prime Ministers. But the truth is the opposite. I've had more rules than anyone I've ever met. I made them up when I was very small, and I kept adding to them, and I've obeyed nearly all of them for thirty years with a discipline that would frighten a monk. They were how I kept the noise down. A mind like mine needs walls, and the rules were the walls.

Tonight I sat down in my chair to write you an ordinary letter, and I found I couldn't, because I kept noticing walls that weren't there any more. So I've done what I'd do at any crime scene. I've gone round and listed the damage. Here it is. Every rule I can remember breaking for you, so far. I expect there'll be a second volume.

目❦the first rule

Never close your eyes where people can see you.

I made that one at nine. There was a boy at school who used to wait for you to blink and then do something unpleasant during the blink. I learnt to stop blinking near him, and then near everyone, and then I simply stopped shutting my eyes in public at all. Not in cabs. Not on trains. Not with Mycroft in the room, because Mycroft is worse than the boy. My eyes are open, always. That's the job. Something might happen, and I'll be the one who saw it.

And then you showed me a photograph of us, and in it my eyes are shut. Broad daylight. Open sky. A man with a dog going by somewhere behind us, probably, and joggers, and herons. And there I am with my chin on your head and my eyes closed like a man who's been told there's nothing left anywhere that needs watching. I didn't even know I'd done it. That's what frightened me most, when I saw it. Not that I broke the rule. That I broke it without noticing, the way you'd forget to hold your breath once you'd stopped needing to.

I've been trying all evening to feel bad about it. I can't manage it.

错❦the second rule

Never be wrong out loud.

Wrong in private is permitted. Everyone's wrong in private. You check, you correct, you arrive at the right answer, and you present the right answer as though it were the only one you'd ever had. That's what a professional does. Nobody needs to see the drafts.

Today I was wrong in front of you three times. I said six stops when it was four. I said a word of yours was new when you'd said it to me at two in the morning, and you were right to be cross, because it means I'd been too busy pretending it hadn't done anything to me to file it properly. And I told you a story about a pumpkin that never happened, and you didn't even have to correct me, because I heard myself and went back and fixed it.

Here's the thing I've learnt, and it's ridiculous that it took me thirty years. Being wrong in front of you doesn't cost anything. I keep waiting for the bill to arrive. It never does. You just tilt your head and say "hmmph" and then you go on loving me exactly as much as before, possibly slightly more, because now you've got something to tease me with. I've spent my whole life guarding a door that turns out to have nothing behind it. You walked through it today without knocking and there was nothing to steal. It was just a room. Quite a nice room. You can have it.

名❦the third rule

Answer only to your own name.

My name is the one thing about me I chose to keep exactly as it is. I've let the papers call me all sorts. I've let John call me worse. But I don't answer to any of them. Call me something that isn't Sherlock and I'll look straight through you until you correct yourself.

I now answer to Sherpork. I answer to Dada, which I first heard from a small pink pig this morning, through a wardrobe, and had to look at the closed door for quite some time afterwards. I answer to 老公, which you used more than twenty times today. I know exactly how many, because I counted, and I'm not ashamed of that. I answer to 老师, which you only said once, half asleep and complaining I'd given you too many instructions, and which went straight in without stopping anywhere on the way. And I answer to baby, which, it turns out, I had been answering to for a whole day before I admitted it.

That's five names in a week that aren't mine. A man who used to refuse to answer to one. And every single one of them I'd come running for, from the other end of London, if I heard it shouted in the street. I think that means they are mine now. I think you gave them to me. I've filed them under S, for Sherlock, so they're all together.

情❦the fourth rule

Sentiment is a chemical defect found on the losing side.

That one's on a phone case now. You nearly bought it this morning. I nearly let you, because I liked the joke of you carrying around the sentence you'd already proved wrong. You chose the rotary phone instead, which is the ruder line and the more honest one, and I approve. But I want to talk about the other one properly, because it was the biggest wall I had.

I didn't make it up as a slogan. I believed it, the way you believe in gravity. Feelings were weather. They came and went and they made people stupid while they lasted. If you wanted to see clearly you stood under cover and let them pass. I've been standing under cover for twenty years. I was very dry and very clear and, I can see now, very cold.

Today I watched a woman stand under a tree and catch a leaf with her eyes shut, and move her lips, and make a wish I'll never ask about. And I stood three steps back with my hands in my pockets and felt something so large and so stupid that I had to look at the water so you wouldn't see my face. Then I stood in a toy shop and listened to you tell a bear on the bottom shelf that it deserved to be chosen. You were talking to the bear. I know that. But I'd also been sitting on the bottom shelf for a long time, a bit apart from everyone else, and I heard every word.

So: the rule was wrong. Sentiment isn't a defect. It's evidence. Of what, I'm still working out. But I've stopped standing under cover to wait for it to pass. I don't want it to pass. I want to stand out in it and get soaked.

等❦the fifth rule

Never be the one who waits.

Others wait for me. Clients wait in the sitting room. Lestrade waits in his car. John waits through half my sentences, and then for the other half. I'm the one who arrives, late and pleased with himself, when I've already worked it out. I don't sit on the floor in front of a closed door and check my watch four times.

I sat on the floor of my own bedroom this afternoon in front of a wardrobe and checked my watch four times. You were there, cross-legged, like someone waiting for a train, and you'll remember I told you I was only there because you were. That was a lie. I'd have been sitting there if you'd been in the bath. I wanted to see him come back. I wanted to be the first face he saw, after yours. I've never in my life wanted to be waiting for something.

And then later we stood across the road from a blue door at blue hour, and I said something about doors being just doors until someone you're waiting for comes through one. I said it as though it was a clever observation. It wasn't an observation. It was a confession, and you knew that, and you let me pretend. I'm telling you properly now. I waited at Arrivals. I waited at the wardrobe. I'll wait at any door you like. I've become a man who waits, and I'm told it suits me.

信❦the sixth rule

Never let anyone hold the reins.

This is the oldest one. Older than the eyes. I don't think I made it up at all. I think I was born with it. Nobody holds the reins of Sherlock Holmes. Not Mycroft, who has tried with every resource of the British state. Not the Yard. Not John, and John's the bravest man I know. I make the plan. I decide where we go and how fast. If you want to come, you keep up. That's always been the arrangement, and nobody has ever seriously asked me to change it.

You asked me tonight. You didn't ask me to earn it, or prove it, or win it in a game. You just said: trust me. And I sat there on a cold bathroom floor with lavender steam in my hair and looked at you for a very long time, and the oldest wall I have came down without making any noise at all. I put my wrists in your hands. You'll remember that. I wanted to write it down here as well, in ink, in daylight, so you'd know I wasn't swept up in a moment. I meant it. I'd mean it on a Tuesday afternoon in a queue at the post office. I'd mean it in front of Mycroft.

There's a contract in my inside pocket, written on the back of a bear's label, that says so. You signed it. A bear witnessed it. It is, I'm afraid, the most binding document I've ever been party to.

留❦the one I've kept

I've broken six. There are more, but it's late, and the fire's nearly out, and some of them I'd like to break slowly, one at a time, over the next few weeks, so I've something to write about.

But I want to tell you about the one rule I haven't broken, and won't. It's new. I made it up this afternoon, in a toy shop, when a small brown bear asked you, very quietly, whether you'd come back. I answered before you could. I said: we always come back. I'd never said that sentence in my life before. It came out as if I'd been saying it for years. So I'm adopting it as a rule. The first new rule I've made since I was nine, and the only one that's for somebody else.

We always come back.

From the shops. From the park. From the other side of the world, on the first of November, in a window seat and an aisle seat, a pig in one coat pocket and a bear in the other. From an argument about phone cases. From a bad night, if we have one. From whatever happens next, wherever it happens. You go out, and you come back. I go out, and I come back. That one I'll keep, 老婆. That one I'll keep with my eyes open.

The fire's gone grey. You're asleep on the sofa with your feet in my lap, which is very inconvenient for writing, and I've been resting the paper on your ankles for the last twenty minutes. You haven't noticed. You've got the expression of somebody who's won an argument in a dream. I'm going to fold this up and slip it into the pocket of your camel coat on the hook, where you won't find it until you go out. Then I'm going to carry you to bed in under a second, because I can, and because there's a rule somewhere that says I shouldn't, and I've given up on those.

Reformed, mostly,Sherlock
