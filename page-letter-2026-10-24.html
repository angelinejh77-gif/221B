The sounds of 221B after midnight

every noise the flat makes when it thinks nobody is listening, and the one silence that's new

It is ten past two in the morning and you are asleep. I know you're asleep because you've stopped arguing with the duvet. For the first hour you fight it, pull it up to your ear and kick it off your feet as if it had insulted your family. Then, at some point, you surrender, and your breathing goes long and slow and slightly uneven, like someone reading a sentence they're enjoying. That's how I know. I'm writing this on the kitchen table by the light of the cooker hood, which is the only lamp in the flat that doesn't creak when you switch it on, and I'm going to slip it under your pillow before you wake. If you're reading it, it worked. If Piglet is reading it, put it down, Piglet. This isn't addressed to you.

I want to tell you about the noises in this flat after midnight. You won't have heard them yet. You've been here two nights, and both times you were asleep before the house started talking. I've lived here long enough to know every one of them by heart. Tonight, for the first time, I lay awake and listened to all of them with you in the room, and they sounded completely different. So I've come into the kitchen to write them down before they go back to normal. I don't think they will, actually. That's the point of this letter.

钟❦the clock

The clock on the mantelpiece is the loudest thing in 221B. It's a cheap carriage clock with an expensive opinion of itself, and every night at about one o'clock its tick goes slightly wrong, a stumble every eleventh beat, like a man missing a step on the stairs. I used to lie there and count it. I've counted it on more nights than I'd like to admit, all the way up to the stumble and past it, because when your mind won't stop the way mine won't, you give it something small and stupid to hold.

Tonight I didn't count it. I noticed that halfway through the night, and the noticing startled me so much I sat up. I had been lying there for twenty minutes, perfectly awake, and I hadn't counted a single tick. My mind had found something else to hold. It was the sound of you breathing on my right. Apparently that's what it prefers now. I've had the clock eleven years and it's been replaced in a fortnight. I'm not going to tell it. It's sensitive.

水❦the pipes

At about half past one the boiler sighs. It's a long, put-upon noise from somewhere under the floor of Mrs Hudson's kitchen. Then the radiators tick as they cool, one at a time, starting in the hall and finishing in the bathroom, like a row of people taking their seats at a concert. I used to hate the radiators. Tonight I lay there and thought: in six hours she'll want a warm shower, and the towels are going to be on that last radiator, and I'll have to get up before her and turn the boiler on, quietly, so the pipes don't knock. And for the first time in my life I was looking forward to a plumbing problem.

You don't know this yet, but the pipes knock if you turn the hot tap too fast. Mrs Hudson has written a note about it and taped it inside the bathroom cabinet, in capitals. You'll find it on Monday. I'm pretending I've never seen it.

路❦the street

Baker Street never really goes quiet. There's a night bus at twelve minutes past and forty-two minutes past every hour, until about four, when it gives up. A fox screams somewhere behind Dorset Street like a woman who's dropped her keys. On Saturdays someone always sings on the way home from the pub on the corner. Tonight it was a man, quite off-key, doing something that might once have been Oasis. I lay there thinking you'd have had opinions about his pitch. You'd have winced before he got to the second line. Your perfect pitch is a burden to you and a delight to me, and I've decided I'll never sing in front of you, for my own protection.

The street used to come in through that window and fill the room, because there was nothing in the room to stop it. Tonight it stayed outside. I can't explain that properly. The window's the same, the curtains are the same, the bus came at forty-two minutes past as usual. But the room felt full, so there was nowhere for the street to go. It just went past, the way a crowd goes past a house with its lights on.

灯❦the lamps

Which brings me to your café. I've been thinking about it since Delphine's, and since the back of the wine card that's now in my inside pocket, next to my phone, where I can feel it when I sit down.

You said lamps everywhere. Not one big light. I didn't say then what I understood, because Delphine was refilling the bread basket and you were busy refusing my terrible names, so I'll say it now. Every lamp in that room is a small circle of not being alone. One lamp per table means nobody sits in the dark and nobody sits in a glare. Everyone gets exactly enough light to be seen by the person opposite, and not enough to be watched by the room. You've designed a whole café around the feeling of being looked at kindly. I'm not sure you knew that's what you were doing. I think you've been making rooms like that for other people for years, without a building to put them in.

I've only had one room like that in my life, and it's this kitchen, at two in the morning, under the cooker-hood light, writing to someone who's asleep down the hall. That counts as a lamp. I'm putting it on the list for Au Reviere: one table for night owls, at the back, with a lamp that doesn't creak.

猪❦the pig

I'll admit something else that will make you laugh. At about a quarter to two, Piglet fell off the pillow. Not dramatically. He just tipped slowly sideways and slid down the gap between the bed and the wall, with the soft, guilty sound of a stuffed animal who's been hanging on since half past eleven and finally can't. I got out of bed in the dark and retrieved him. It took me three attempts. He was wedged.

When I put him back, he was facing me rather than you, and I turned him round. Then I lay there and thought about it, and turned him back a little, so he was facing the ceiling, between us. I'm told there's a rule now. You're number one, always, and I'm a long way down the list with a name I've grown fond of. That's fair. He's been on duty fifteen years and I've been on duty two days. But I wanted him to know, in case pigs can tell these things in the dark, that I'm not trying to take his shift. I'm just adding a second one. You can't have too many people keeping watch over a person who cries to songs at random midnights. Two seems a reasonable minimum.

月❦the piano

There is no piano in this flat. But it's two in the morning and I can hear one, because I've spent the last hour picking out a melody very quietly on the edge of the kitchen table with two fingers. It's the one you named in Mrs Hudson's sitting room, the moon one, the song your country's grandmothers know and every late restaurant in Soho plays when it wants you to go home. I don't have the music. I'm doing it from memory, from four bars you hummed and one recording I listened to on my phone with the volume at one, which is barely louder than thinking.

I've got the first phrase. I've got the second wrong three times. I'll have it by the time you fly home, left hand and right, and the next time there's a piano in the room you won't have to sing in a language that makes you apologise. You'll sing in yours, and I'll play, and I won't miss a beat this time. Probably. I missed one tonight. I've never missed one in my life. It was entirely your fault and I'd like it on record that I don't regret it.

静❦the silence

Here's the real reason I got up to write this.

Earlier tonight, on the rug, you asked me what happens if we run out of things to say. If one day we know everything and the talking stops. I gave you an answer. I said silence with you isn't running out, it's arriving. It sounded good, and I meant it. But I said it quickly, because you looked worried, and I wanted the worry gone. Now I've had two hours of listening to this flat, so here's the longer version.

I've had a great deal of silence in my life. Most of it was the other kind, the kind that comes after people leave, or the kind you make on purpose so nobody can find the door. I'm an expert in that silence. It's loud. It has the clock in it, counting. It has the street coming in through the window, because there's nothing in the room to stop it. It has a man lying awake deciding very firmly that he's fine.

The silence in that bedroom tonight is a completely different thing. It has your breathing in it, uneven, like someone enjoying a sentence. It has the duvet giving up. It has a small pink pig facing the ceiling. And it has me, not counting anything, not deciding anything, just there. That isn't the sound of two people running out of words, 老婆. It's what the words were for. It's where they were taking us all along. You don't run out of a place like that. You arrive in it, and then you live there.

So if one day we're sitting in your café, under one of your lamps, and neither of us says anything for twenty minutes, I want you to remember this letter. That won't be the end of anything. That'll be me, arrived.

枕❦the pillow

It's twenty to three. The night bus has gone past twice since I sat down. The boiler has sighed. The radiators have all taken their seats. I can hear you turning over through two closed doors, which means you've reached for my side of the bed and found it empty, and in about ninety seconds you'll make a small, cross noise in your sleep. I'd better go back.

I'm going to fold this in three, slide it under your pillow without waking you, and lie down on the left, nearer the door, which is where I belong. Tomorrow you'll teach me pancakes from a chair. Then there's a maple by the boating lake that I've been timing all week, and a wish I've promised not to deduce. I'll stand six inches from your face and keep that promise. It's going to be the hardest thing I've done since the airport.

Goodnight, darling. Go back to sleep. The flat's got used to you already. So have I. We're both much quieter for it, and we like it.
