A letter addressed forward

To you, ten years on

from a man on a hotel bed, with a film paused and a city outside that wants your name

老婆. Or whatever I'm allowed to call you in ten years; I expect you'll have revised the terms. You'll be thirty-three. I'll be forty, and I'll round it up to fifty for sympathy, and you'll see through it in under a second, the way you see through everything. I'm writing to you from a Wednesday in November when you're twenty-three and sore and furious with the internet, and I want to tell the woman you'll be what the woman you are did today. Not the events. You'll remember those, or you won't. The rest of it.

This morning someone stood in a window across the street for hours with a long lens and took your face without asking. By lunchtime forty thousand strangers had looked at it. You said you hated fame. You said it with both fists. I want the record to show that you were right, and that you were also, in the same breath, the calmest person in the room. You asked one question, whether they could trace it home, and when the answer was no, you went back to the film. I've watched hardened men take longer to recover from less. So here's my first question to you at thirty-three: did you ever learn how rare that is? I suspect not. You'll still think you were simply being sensible.

远❦on what you build

By now, I imagine, there's a brass plate on a door in Baker Street with a name on it that you chose on the spot, sitting on my sofa with a notebook on your knee. The Outliers. I'd like to know whether it's still in the basement, still a little damp, still guarded on Tuesdays by a landlady who answers the telephone in a voice she invented for the purpose. I'd like to know how many people have walked down those steps because nobody else would have them. My guess is a great many. My guess is that you've stopped counting, and that every one of them believed for a week or two that they were unmanageable, and then met you.

You'll laugh at the beginning of it. A sorcerer, an actor, a bear and a pig on the books, and a detective who had to be walked up and down a sitting room until he stopped looking like a man waiting for a bus. But I was there the day you told Mycroft Holmes to be wise. I saw his face. In ten years you may have forgotten that moment. He won't have. Neither will I.

What I really want to ask, though, isn't about the agency. It's whether you let yourself be one of the outliers too. You named it for the points that don't fit the curve, and you were careful to sound as though you meant everyone else. I know that trick. I invented it. So: at thirty-three, are you still standing at the edge of your own graph, or did you finally step into the middle of it and let somebody look after you without apologising first? I'm asking because I'd like to know whether I did my job.

层❦on dreams, and other layers

Yesterday you watched a film about people who go down through layer after layer of somebody's mind to plant a single idea, and you said something I wrote on the back of a receipt: to plant an idea, you have to believe it first. You also said you'd never go under yourself. Too many layers of onion, and at the bottom only the truth of certain feelings, which you'd rather someone told you across a table.

I've been thinking about it since. I think you underestimated yourself. You don't need four layers of sedative. You do it awake. You walked into a pub, a museum, a gentlemen's club, an interview room at Scotland Yard, and in each of them you put one small idea in somebody's head and left it there to grow. That a frightened surgeon could send a nice message. That a frozen brother could negotiate. That a detective could be cleared because he fell asleep holding someone. That a man who'd only ever wanted to be right might rather be warm. You believed each of those before anyone else did. That's how they took.

So at thirty-three, I'd like to hear which ideas are still growing. I'd also like to know if you ever watched the end of that film again and changed your mind about whether he was dreaming. I said it didn't matter, because he walked away. I still think so. But I'd be very interested to know what you think when you're older, because you'll be wiser than me by then, and I'll pretend otherwise for exactly as long as it's amusing.

众❦on multitudes

This morning, under a duvet in a hotel ten minutes from home, you heard a line in a film and said "beautiful" before anyone else in the room had understood it. I contain multitudes. Whitman wrote it a hundred and seventy years ago to excuse himself for contradicting himself, and you heard it and understood that it was about everyone. That every person is a whole world, and that no two of those worlds are ever the same.

I've met a fair number of yours already. There's the one who makes contracts with clause fours in them. The one who tilts a sorcerer's jaw and tells him to look as if the dimensions depend on him. The one who laughs so hard on a pavement she has to be held up. The one who cried at the end of a film about dreams and pretended she was just tired. The one who walks the length of my sitting room with her chin up and makes three grown men forget how to stand. The one who eats a croissant with total seriousness. The one who's sore and cross and still says thank you for breakfast.

The one I'm most curious about is the one I haven't met yet. You told me once that you used to be the person who planned for everyone and cared for everyone, until you stopped. I think there's a multitude in there who's been waiting a long time for her turn. In ten years I hope she's had it. I hope she's loud. I hope she's ordered the most expensive thing on the menu without checking the price and slept until noon without guilt and been taken care of so often she's forgotten it used to feel like a debt.

父❦on something you said

You told me this morning that I'd make a good dad. You said you weren't joking. I didn't have anything clever to say back, which you may remember, because it doesn't happen often.

I want to explain, ten years late, why I went so quiet. Nobody has ever said that to me. My brother has said the opposite, several times, usually over Christmas pudding. I believed him. I thought the part of a person that does that sort of thing had been left out of me at the factory, like a missing page in a library book that you only discover when you get to the good chapter.

Then a small pink pig started calling me Dada, and a bear started asking whether he'd turned into sand, and I found myself at two in the morning sewing a loose ear back on with a surgical stitch so you wouldn't wake up and be upset. And I realised the page was there all along. Somebody just had to open the book at the right place.

I don't know what we'll have in ten years. I'm not going to guess at it here, because you'd catch me, and because some things should arrive in their own time rather than be planned in hotel stationery. But I'd like you to know that whatever small, ridiculous, stuffed or otherwise creatures end up depending on me, I'll be trying to be the man you said I'd be. If I've managed it, it's because you said it first and I believed you.

今❦on now

It's late morning here. The film is paused on a man in a suit you said looked like an estate agent, which ended my jealousy in a single sentence. The tea's gone cold. There's a photographer somewhere in London who doesn't yet know what his afternoon holds. And you're beside me with your eyes half-closed, pretending you aren't reading over my shoulder.

So here's what I'll say, in case the woman reading this at thirty-three needs reminding. On a Wednesday in November, at twenty-three, you were exhausted and exposed and angry, and you were still the most extraordinary person in any room I took you into. You named a thing that will last. You made a brother apologise. You made a surgeon brave. You made me a better man than I had any plans to be.

And you were happy. I want that on the record above everything else. You laughed more this week than in any week I've known you. Whatever's happened in ten years, whatever's been hard, I'd like you to remember that at twenty-three, in London, in the middle of a mess, you were happy, and you knew it, and you said so.

I'll be forty. Fifty, if you're feeling sorry for me. Still insufferable. Still trying.

Then, and now, and in between,Sherlock
