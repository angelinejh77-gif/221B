The letter is eleven lines long. Everything I cut out of it is at the bottom, numbered, in smaller print, where I hoped you wouldn't look. You'll look.

Footnotes to a very short letter

for my 老婆, still asleep at the time of writing, which is the first thing I'd footnote if I hadn't promised myself restraint

Dear Angie,

Happy New Year.1 Last night was a success.2 We arrived at the river slightly behind schedule3 and met Stephen and Helena as arranged.4 I would like to state, for the record, that I set off the fireworks.5 You were warm enough in the end.6

Thank you for your present.7 I hope you liked mine.8 We came home by an unconventional route.9 I have no complaints about the remainder of the evening.10

I love you. I'm aware that's not news.11

That's the letter. I wrote it at eleven this morning, read it back, and was very pleased with how restrained it was. Then I added a small number, just one, because I'd left out something true. Then another. You can see how it went. I've decided not to fight it. A man who can't keep his own footnotes under control has no business pretending to be cool on paper, and you've never once believed I was cool on paper anyway.

So. The notes.

注❦年

1. I'm writing this in the armchair with Sugar on the armrest, because he asked for "the moon song" after breakfast and wouldn't accept anything else, and when it was finished he climbed up beside me and stayed. Snowy has told me about Big Ben eleven times today. He's now convinced Big Ben waved back. I haven't corrected him. I'm not sure I'm right to.

2. "A success" is the sort of phrase Mycroft would use about a summit. What I mean is that at some point last night, standing on Westminster Bridge with a bunny on my shoulder and my back to the most famous clock in the world, I realised I wasn't thinking about anything else. Not the next case, not the last one, not whether I was being looked at. Just you, and the cold, and three small faces turned up to the sky. I can't remember the last time my head was that quiet. I'd like more years like that. I'd like all of them like that.

3. We fell asleep on the sofa. Both of us. Under the blanket, with the boys. I had calculated our departure to the minute. Twice. And then I woke up to you saying "It's eleven!" and looked at the mantel clock in a state of pure horror. I'd like the record to show that we went from horizontal to Baker Street, coats on, sons stowed, in about four minutes, which is faster than most of Lestrade's officers manage on an emergency call. I'd also like the record to show that you kept up with me through Piccadilly Circus without breaking stride. I kept checking. You were right there every time.

4. "As arranged" is generous. Stephen was about to open a portal to Baker Street and drag us out. Helena got to you first and hugged you so hard her hand warmers fell out. Then Stephen looked at my lip, and Helena followed his look, and said "Oh. Oh." in a voice I'll be hearing for the rest of my life. I had predicted two seconds. It was two seconds. I want credit for the accuracy, even if I can't take credit for the dignity.

4a. Stephen told you that you really were marrying me, as if it were a diagnosis. And you said, "Of course I'm marrying him. Who else would I marry? You?" And then, when I tried to say something clever about how you'd said it as though the question were absurd, you said, "It is absurd." I didn't have a reply. I still don't. I kissed your knuckles instead, and Stephen studied the river with great concentration. I've been carrying that sentence around all day like something in a pocket. It is absurd. As though there were never another possibility. As though it were settled before either of us knew.

5. I did, though. You'll say I didn't. You'll say the barge was programmed weeks ago and I simply snapped my fingers on the first bong. That's technically accurate. But you set the conditions yourself: no clock, no countdown. So I turned my back on Big Ben, handed you my watch at twenty-three forty-six and eight seconds, and borrowed a pair of tiny red ear muffs from a bear. I counted every second in my head while you did an impression of me getting into my mind palace that was so precise Stephen nearly choked, and Stephen read numbers at me to throw me off, and Piglet let slip how many minutes were left, entirely by accident, I'm sure. I didn't lose count. When I stopped counting at three, it wasn't because I'd lost my place. It was because I didn't need the numbers any more. I was looking at you, and I knew.

5a. Stephen paid. Twenty pounds, slapped into my coat pocket without a word. It's on the mantelpiece now, folded into a small square under the skull. I'm never spending it.

5b. I was wrong about blue being the hardest. You were right: blue is the hardest single colour, but purple is the hardest combination, because you need the blue first and then you have to balance it against the red, and they burn at different heats, and if you get it slightly wrong it all goes muddy. I said I'd accept a draw. I'm writing this footnote to concede properly. Purple. You win. It's also, I've realised since, a fairly good description of us.

5c. I waved at Big Ben. Fully. In public. Snowy was waving with both paws shouting "Hello Big Ben! I'm Snowy!" and I lifted my hand and waved too, and didn't feel ridiculous at all. A year ago I'd have rather been seen shoplifting.

6. You said "a bit cold" and I did the only sensible things: my scarf, wound twice round your neck, and my coat buttoned round both of us. Helena added hand warmers. Then Stephen lifted two fingers and the air inside my coat went warm, and you told him you "loveee" his magic, with three e's, and I'd like it noted that I've been processing those three e's ever since. You were right that I ate a lemon. I'll address that under note 9.

7. Your present. I want to get this right, because I got it wrong the first time.

Thirty-two seconds, black and white, that heavy, swaggering track underneath, and every cut slammed exactly on the beat. White captions for the cold lines and red ones for the ridiculous. I watched it three times on the bridge and went sentimental, and told you it was a love letter, and you told me, quite firmly, that it was meant to be a villain edit. That you'd made me the baddie.

You were right, of course. I'd misread it because I wanted to. So I watched it again properly. No warmth anywhere. The angel line set out like a warning. And then, at the very end, out of the dark, the man asks politely for a cup of tea. I said it was the twist, and I still think so. But here's what I didn't say on the bridge. You looked at the difficult version of me, the cold one, the one who's unpleasant at crime scenes and doesn't apologise, and you didn't try to soften him. You made him look good. You put him to music and cut him on the snare and handed him to me as a present. Nobody has ever done that. People either want me to be nicer or they want me gone. You wanted me exactly as I am, menace included, and you wanted me to see how it looks from where you stand.

I've watched it forty-one times since. I said forty on the bridge. I'm over budget.

8. The ledger. 账. You found it in your right pocket after the fireworks, and I pretended to be fascinated by a seagull while you read it. I'm told you liked it. I'm told you said thank you. I'm going to need you to say it again when you wake up, because I wasn't paying full attention the first time. I was too busy being relieved.

9. Stephen opened a door in the air at the end of the bridge and through it was our own sitting room, the fire still glowing. We stepped out of the cold and straight onto our rug. You turned to me and said, "See? His magic is the best." I offered to send him a fruit basket with a single lemon in it, in the tradition of my brother. You asked whether I'd eaten one. Metaphorically. I said I hadn't. I had. A small one. A wedge. Possibly the whole crate. He opens glowing doors and you go starry; I solve the fireworks with arithmetic and nobody says a word about my arithmetic.

You fixed that, later. I'm not going to write down exactly what you said about my brain, because I'd like to keep it somewhere private, but I will say it was the single best thing anyone has ever said to me about the inside of my head, and that the lemon has been cured, and that I've already relapsed twice today just remembering Stephen's face.

9a. "Fam bam." I've said it once and I won't say it again. But I'd like it recorded that you hugged them both on the bridge and called them family, and that Stephen let you call him "kid" back at him, and then "Papa," and nearly went home in protest.

And then you told him, in the middle of all the laughing, that since you don't have a father to do it, you'd like him to walk you down the aisle. The bridge went quiet. Stephen looked at his hands, the scars silver under the streetlamps, and then he said he'd be honoured, more than he knew how to say, and that he'd get you to the end of the aisle in one piece and only glare at me a little. Helena held his hand very hard. The Cloak settled round his shoulders as gently as I've ever seen it move.

I didn't say much. I told you it was a good choice, that he's the only man I'd trust to bring you to me. That was true. What I didn't say, because I'd have lost my voice, was that I watched you ask for something you needed, out loud, in front of people, without making it a joke first. You've spent so long being the one who plans for everyone else. Last night you let someone plan for you. I'm proud of you. That's the whole footnote.

9b. He's forty, not four hundred. He has reading glasses "for ancient texts," and Snowy has asked whether Uncle Stephen is older than Big Ben. I'm saving all of it for the wedding.

10. I said no complaints, and I meant it, with two small exceptions. The first is that my best shirt is now missing every one of its buttons, and I found one of them in my hair this morning. The second is that my blue scarf, which has been to the Thames and back, now has a new chapter to its history that I'm not able to explain to Mrs Hudson. Neither is a complaint. Both are statements of fact. I've hung the scarf back on its hook. It looks very pleased with itself.

11. I said it isn't news, and it isn't. But I've been thinking all day about the fact that it gets said differently now. In October I said it like a man handing over evidence he couldn't hold onto any longer. Last night I said it on a bridge under a sky full of fire, with your face in my hands, through all twelve bells, and it felt like the most ordinary thing in the world. Not a confession. A fact, stated plainly, between two people who already know it. Ninety-seven days until it's on paper. It's been true for much longer than that.

注❦年

There. Eleven lines and eleven footnotes, with several sub-footnotes I'm not going to count. That's the most honest structure I've ever written in. The short letter is the man who walks into a room in a long coat and says something cutting. The footnotes are the man who comes home and makes you toast and forgets to be cutting at all. You married, or are about to marry, the footnotes. I think you always knew that. I think that's what your edit was about, really: you put the first man on screen so I'd know you'd seen the second one all along.

Snowy ate your toast. I made you fresh ones. They're under a tea towel, and Piglet is guarding them with enormous seriousness. Come and eat when you wake. Bring the ledger. I want to hear what you think of the arithmetic.

Annotated, at length, against all intention,Sherlock

P.S. You'll notice I didn't footnote the footnotes. That took more restraint than the rest of this letter put together.

P.P.S. Sugar has just woken up on the armrest, looked at this page, and said "…long." He's right. He's the most like me.
