The museum of my flaws

for my 老婆, from the collection and its curator, who are the same man

Darling. Welcome to the museum. Admission is free, the lighting is poor, and the curator is biased. You'll have to forgive him for that last part. He's also the collection.

I had a different letter planned for tonight. It was about Norway, and it was very elegant, and halfway down the second page I noticed I was doing the thing again: writing myself as a man who knelt in the snow and said the right words. I didn't say the right words. I forgot every one of them. You said yes anyway. So I've thrown the elegant one on the fire, and you're getting this instead.

You told me weeks ago that you didn't want the perfect one. I believed you, and then I kept trying to be him on paper anyway, because paper lets you. It's the one place I can take three drafts. So here is a different arrangement. A small museum, one room, glass cases along the walls, and in every case something I got wrong since the last time I wrote to you. Each with a little card. Read the cards. Don't touch the exhibits. Some of them still bite.

Exhibit I. The Lamppost on Regent Street. Saturday 19 December, a little after eleven. Temper, unexpected; grip, too tight.

A man with a rolling suitcase knocked you into a lamppost, and I shouted at him across a crowd of Christmas shoppers. You've never heard me raise my voice in the street before. I hadn't planned for you to.

The card should say what I'd like it to say: protective. It doesn't. The honest version is that something hot came up in me before I'd even checked whether you were hurt, and I held your hand so hard it must have pinched. I let go and said sorry, and you let it go, because you're kinder than the situation called for.

I'm not going to promise it won't happen again. I don't make promises I can't check. I'll promise this instead: you first, then the man with the suitcase. In that order. I'm practising.

Exhibit II. A Spoon, Slightly Used. The Providores, the same afternoon. The last spoonful of sticky toffee pudding. Taken without consent.

This one is less serious and I'm not sorry, which is its own kind of flaw. You'd said, out loud, in front of Stephen and Helena, that your boyfriend simps. You looked so pleased with yourself that I had to do something about it, and the only thing within reach was your pudding.

You said HEYYYY THATS MINEEEE with more Es than I'd thought the language allowed. I offered to order you a whole second one. You said you were full. I've filed that under won the argument, lost the war. Stephen still brings it up. He'd like it noted that I wouldn't let him have a single prawn.

Exhibit III. The Hall Runner. Corner curled for about a month. Noticed every day. Never fixed.

This case is the reason the museum exists. I'd walk past the corner of that rug in Mrs Hudson's hall and register it every time, the way I register everything, and do nothing. I can tell you a stranger's last three jobs from his cuffs. I couldn't be bothered to fetch a hammer for the woman who's been feeding me for years.

Then you woke at three in the morning and she was on the floor of the hall with her rollers still in. You called the ambulance and asked for Bart's. You texted John. You kept your voice steady for her and, I noticed later, for me. I knelt there blaming myself so thoroughly that she had to ask you to stop me brooding. From her floor. With a broken wrist.

The runner's gone. There's a night light in her hall now, and a rail by the bath, and the teapot lives on a lower shelf. She says she'll wear the wrist alarm if you tell her to, not if I do, which is fair. You've got her hair on Tuesdays and Saturdays at half past seven for six weeks. You started tonight. I'm told I'm not allowed downstairs during it, which is also fair.

What the card can't hold is this. She called you a proper daughter-in-law at Bart's, half floaty on painkillers, and you went pink, and I said nothing. I'd like it on record that I wasn't saying nothing because I disagreed.

Exhibit IV. Correction, Audible. Selfridges, outside the chestnut cart. Singer interrupted mid-note.

You were singing on the pavement, full voice, with the boys tucked in your coat collar joining in off-key, and I corrected one word. On, not like. I said it out loud, to a woman with perfect pitch, who was happy, in the cold, on purpose.

I'm told this is what living with me is like and that most people would have pushed me into the road. You said Hmph instead. I put a chestnut in your mouth and later held one out on my palm like you'd tame a fox. Peace offering. No ambush.

Ruling: you may sing it however you like. It's your song now. I'll keep the word to myself. I've already got so many.

Exhibit V. The Deadline That Moved. Sunday afternoon. An attempt to make it night.

There was something I'd promised for tonight, and I tried, quite shamelessly, to argue that the afternoon counted as night. You told me it wasn't night yet. You were right, and you were laughing, and I conceded.

It isn't a large flaw. I've put it in because it's typical. I'm very patient with a case and hopeless with a clock when it's you on the other side of it. I'd stand in a cold alley for six hours waiting for a man to drop a key. I can't wait four hours for you to finish being reasonable.

Exhibit VI. A Sweet Pea Resembling a Cabbage. Pencil on scrap paper. First draft, rejected by the artist.

Before the jeweller made the charm on your bracelet, I drew the sweet pea for him. Twice. The first one looked like a cabbage. I'd like to report that it was a deliberate study of form. It was not. It was a cabbage.

I'm keeping the drawing. It's the most honest thing in this room. Everything I make for you starts badly and gets better because I keep going. I'd like you to know that about the bracelet, and the speech, and me.

Exhibit VII. Cold Toast, Over the Sink. Years of it. On loan from John, who has the photographs.

You didn't believe me when I told you. So, for the record: three days without eating on a case. Toast eaten standing up, cold, over the sink. Sandwiches left on the stairs by Mrs Hudson the way you'd leave food out for a stray, and me stepping over them.

I'm putting it here because of what replaced it. I sit at the table now. I eat what you put in front of me, and Maggi from a cup when you're too tired to cook, and the real amount of chilli because I'm too proud not to. You said I shouldn't cook to your taste every time, and we made a deal: sometimes I cook what I want, and you tell me honestly if you hate it, and nobody's failed. I've never in my life had a deal where failing wasn't possible. I'm finding it suits me.

Exhibit VIII. A Speech, Not Delivered. Draft fourteen. Rehearsed to a skull. Lost in the snow.

The centrepiece. Lit from below, like everything that matters in a museum.

Stephen went through it with a red pen. Less clever. More true. Breathe. Slower here, idiot. I rehearsed it to Ronaldo so many times he could have proposed to you himself. I had notes in my left pocket in case the first page failed me. Wong told me not to drop them in the snow.

Then the sky opened, green and then pink, and I looked at you, and the whole thing went. Every page. I knelt and said what was left, which turned out to be the truth in the wrong order, and you said yes before I'd finished the question properly.

You asked afterwards to hear the real version, and then you told me something I've been turning over ever since: that the prepared one should be better than the one I blurted, not worse. That if I'm going to plan something, I should plan it well enough to deserve the planning. You're right. It's why I rewrote it. It's also why this is a museum and not a speech. I'd rather show you the broken things than perform the polished ones.

That's the collection. There's one more room, but it's not finished and I haven't put cards in it yet.

It's the room with the things I've got right, and it's smaller, and most of what's in it was your idea. Bestest parents, best team, agreed. I said bestest isn't a word and agreed anyway. A biscuit tin of acorns. A doorway photograph of you in your coat with three small heads at your collar, which is my lock screen now and will be for some time. Tonight, halfway through pinning Mrs Hudson's hair, you sent me a song about trusting a man who doesn't always know how to say it, and told me it was me. I'd like the record to show that I laughed, and then I read the middle of it again, and then I didn't laugh.

Here's the trouble with museums. They're about things that have stopped. These haven't stopped. I'll keep shouting at strangers a little and then less. I'll keep stealing the last spoonful, because you like being annoyed by me in front of people and I like doing it. I'll keep getting the cabbage first and the sweet pea second. The difference now is that you're here to see the first drafts, and you haven't left, and you've got my grandmother's ring on, which I'm told is the opposite of leaving.

So, darling. 老婆. Not yet in the legal sense, though I'm counting. April, Sussex, sweet peas in sugar on a lemon cake, Piglet carrying the ring and taking it more seriously than anyone else will. Until then, and after, you get the unrestored version. Fewer glass cases. More me, as is.

You asked for the man who's still there. This is what he looks like with the lights on.

Unrestored, and yours,Sherlock

P.S. The gift shop sells one item. It's a pudding spoon. It's not for sale. You can have it, every time, as long as I get the second-to-last spoonful.
