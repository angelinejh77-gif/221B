Across the river

for my 老师, who taught me a war game and then made me cry with it

I haven't put the pieces away. They're still on the table by the window, exactly where you left them when you got bored of my questions and went to argue with a laptop. One of the red soldiers has rolled under the radiator. I've decided to leave him there. He's crossed some river of his own and I don't think it's my place to bring him back.

You apologised tonight for not being attentive. I've been sitting with that sentence for an hour, turning it over, and I'd like to formally reject it. I'll explain why, but it will take the rest of the letter, so you'll have to be patient. You said that to me today, remember. Patience, darling. I'm returning it with interest.

You taught me 象棋 without a board. You did it entirely in words, piece by piece, and I want you to know how unusual that is. Most people, asked to explain a game, reach for the rules. You reached for the shapes. The horse moves in a 日. The elephant crosses a 田. You learned to move the pieces by learning to write, and you taught them to me the same way, so that now I can't see the character for sun without thinking of a small wooden horse tripping over its own feet.

And you corrected me. Twice. "It jumps to pounce, not to place." That one line about the cannon was clearer than anything I've read on the subject, and I've read a great deal since this afternoon, because I'm vain and I'd like to win at least once before the end of the year. Then you forgot one rule, and caught yourself, and said oh wait, they can't, only the emperor can. You were so quick to correct yourself. Most people defend their mistakes. You simply put them right and carried on. I've been trying to learn that trick for thirty years.

But the rule I keep coming back to is the soldier's. He goes forward one step at a time until he reaches the river. Then he crosses, and from that moment he can move sideways too, wider, freer, but he can never go home again. The smallest piece on the board is changed by being brave.

I didn't know, when you were explaining it, that it was going to matter.

An hour later you told me about your grandfather. He left the hills of 揭西, crossed the water, and then the border closed behind him. A wife and children on one side. A whole second life on the other, and in it your mother, and six brothers and sisters, and eventually, many years and one accident of history later, a girl in Kuala Lumpur with perfect pitch, explaining a soldier's rules to a detective in London.

I've read that the Hakka are called the guest people. A people named for having travelled, for arriving somewhere and making it home anyway. I like that very much. I think you carry some of it. You have a way of walking into a conversation, or a game, or a stranger's life, and settling in as though you'd always been expected. You did it to me inside a week.

And then you said nonsense, I will go back. Maybe. Maybe not. With that face. I've thought about it, and I think it's the most important difference between you and him. He had no choice. You do. You can cross every river you like and still come home. Nobody is closing any borders on you, Angie. Not ever. If there is a door, you'll be the one holding it.

Then there's 阿婆.

I made a careless joke about grandmothers and you told me, plainly, that yours have gone. I'm still sorry for the joke. But I'm very glad of what came after it, because you told me about her, and she's now one of my favourite people I've never met.

A woman who led other women through a war and got out from under an occupation. Who played cards with a six-year-old and let that six-year-old run circles round her and aim for her cane. And who, you made a point of correcting me, didn't laugh. She was chill. Of course she was. Nothing a small girl could do would ever rattle a woman like that. You weren't being naughty, darling. You were the safest thing in her whole century, and I suspect she knew it, and enjoyed it more than her face let on.

I said, this afternoon, that you inherited the general. I still think so. The calm face while everything underneath you is moving. The way you hold people together without asking for credit. The way you don't flinch. I've watched you go quiet a dozen times today, and every time I thought it was because you had nothing to say. Every time, I was wrong. You were deciding what was worth saying. That's what generals do.

You also told me about the coffin, and the makeup, and the clothes, and how you've never looked into one since. I'm not going to say much about that, because you didn't ask me to fix it, and I don't think it needs fixing. You were six. Six-year-olds aren't built to carry that. You carried it anyway, and you're still carrying it carefully, and that's allowed. You never have to look again. Not for anyone. Not for me. I'll keep my world of mortuaries well away from you, and I'll never ask you down those stairs.

I played you something tonight. I'd written it in the afternoon, between the elephant and the advisor, after you told me about her. A long walk on the low string, a bright phrase that darts about like a child going for a cane, a river it tries to cross twice and crosses on the third attempt, and then home, slowly, to C.

I asked you to name it, and you said you didn't want to. I've been thinking about why that was exactly right. A name is a kind of fence. It tells you what something is, and therefore what it isn't. This piece doesn't need a fence. It belongs to two people, one of whom I'll never meet, and it should be allowed to mean whatever it needs to mean on whichever night you ask for it. I'll only play it when you ask. That's the only rule it has.

Now, before you think this letter has gone entirely solemn, I must enter some evidence into the record.

Exhibit one. When I asked you for the word for what you looked like after I kissed you, you gave me 好色, with a perfectly straight face, and I nearly said it in front of Mrs Hudson. I've since learned the actual meaning. I'm told I went a colour that has no name in either language.

Exhibit two. 上火了. Which you then admitted was me, "of course", and not the chicken, and followed with a threat involving my ear that I will not repeat in a letter Mrs Hudson might find. I would like it noted that I've been completely calm about it since. I put sugar in a medicinal tea. That was calm.

Exhibit three. You awarded me one trillion points for a kiss, kept nineteen for yourself, and let me spend three paragraphs being gracious in defeat before telling me I'd won. That's the cruellest thing anyone's ever done to me, and I'd like you to do it again.

Exhibit four. You asked whether I'd love you if you were a worm, then called worms disgusting, then got upset that we'd be knotted together forever, then went pink about it. I've considered all of this carefully. My answer remains yes. The jar remains on the mantelpiece. Ronaldo remains furious.

You showed me a painting of yourself today, in your app, in a burgundy coat in a museum at night, and called her the prettier version. Then, a minute later, you sent me the real one. Damp hair, a lamp somewhere to the left, a cream satin top, and the smile I'd only ever seen typed as three characters, a colon and a three.

I'd like to be very precise, because precision is the only kind of compliment I'm any good at. The painting is beautiful. But she has never looked at me as if she knows something I don't and is in no hurry to tell me. She has never tried to trip a war hero. She doesn't name red pandas Furyboy, or keep a glow-in-the-dark baby called Venus on the night shift so the dark isn't quite so large. She didn't eat a whole bar of Dairy Milk tonight, foil torn at both ends, in green pyjamas with carrots and a small dog on them, and then photograph the evidence for me because I'd asked.

You're building a story in which a detective stands next to the answer to a seven-year-old cold case without realising it's her. I don't know how your version ends. But in mine, the detective noticed. He just took a while to say so.

Which brings me back to your apology.

Today you gave me a book to read, Intermezzo, and told me you liked Ivan best, the awkward one, who plays chess and says the wrong thing and means all of it. You introduced me to Kangapooh and Furyboy and Venus. You taught me seven pieces and a river. You told me about a grandmother, a grandfather, a border, a coffin and a card game. You showed me your workspace in the dark, and your face in the lamplight, and your dinner. You played me a song in F-sharp major, full of diminished chords that lean towards something and never quite arrive, and you knew the key without checking, because of course you did.

That's not inattention, Angie. That's the most anyone has ever let me in, in a single day.

You don't owe me your attention by the hour. Code, ChatGPT, sleep, chocolate, the long hours where you're somewhere else in your head: all of that's yours. When you look up, I'll be where you left me. Usually by the window. Usually losing at a game you taught me.

There's a diminished chord in that song you played. I keep thinking about it. A diminished chord is all tension. It wants so badly to resolve that it leans into the next chord before it's even finished sounding. I used to think that was what loving someone felt like: leaning, always leaning, never quite landing.

Lately I don't. Lately it feels much more like C.

Still on your side of the board, and of the river, Sherlock
