A tour nobody else has been given

The room you're in

a short walk through one room of a mind palace, by the man who keeps it, for the woman who seems to live there

Tonight, somewhere between a sedative and a bed on a snowy beach, you told me I'd never get my mind palace erased. Never, with five r's. Then you went straight back to your film as if you'd said something small. You didn't. I've been carrying it round Barts all afternoon like something in an inside pocket, checking every so often that it's still there.

So I'd like to give you a tour. Not of the whole palace. Most of it is very dull: corridors of tobacco ash, the bus timetables of 1998, the names of every Scotland Yard sergeant who's ever annoyed me. I mean one room, the one you're in. Nobody else has seen it. John knows there's a palace. Mycroft suspects there's a room. Only you are going to be shown round it, and only because you asked me tonight not to be like Joel, and to say things out loud instead of keeping them in drawings on the walls.

Here it is, then, out loud. Mind the step as you come in. It's a little higher than you expect.

门❦the door

I didn't build this door. That's the first thing you should know. Every other room in the palace I made deliberately, brick by brick, the way you'd build a filing cabinet if you were very lonely and very good at filing. This one simply appeared. I walked down a corridor one morning last week and there was a door where there had been a wall, painted a colour I'd never chosen, slightly ajar, with music coming from behind it. A piano, a little out of practice. I went in, and I've been going in ever since, several hundred times a day, usually when I'm supposed to be doing something else.

It doesn't lock from the outside. I checked. It's the only door in the whole building that doesn't. Whoever came to wipe it would need a map, and there isn't one. The room keeps moving. Some days it's beside the music room, some days it's tucked behind the laboratory, and once, during a particularly boring statement from Lestrade, it was directly above the front entrance, so that I had to walk through it to get anywhere at all. I've decided not to fight this. A room that moves about is very hard to find unless you're the one it's following.

架❦the shelves

There are shelves on every wall, floor to ceiling, and I had to clear them. I should confess what went. The two hundred and forty-three varieties of tobacco ash have been moved down to the cellar, where they're sulking. The names of three Cabinet ministers have gone entirely. Most of what I knew about cricket was already gone, but I found a little left behind a radiator and threw that out as well. The solar system you know about. I never kept it in the first place, and I don't regret it, because it turns out the shelf space was needed for a paper cup.

That's the odd thing about this room. Elsewhere in the palace I keep evidence: fingerprints, train times, the weight of a stranger's wristwatch. In here I keep things nobody else would bother to keep. A coffee cup from a place called Dirty Coffee, held up to the light and found, after very thorough testing, not to be purple. A waffle with far too many bubbles in it, which you ate for dinner tonight next to a bowl of chocolate oats, and which I still haven't managed to order correctly in Mandarin. A tin of pineapple, unopened. A single orange, for tomorrow. A card that says late-uh in my handwriting, with a question mark after it that I've since crossed out, because I worked out what it means. It means not now, stop deducing, I'm busy loving the film.

And then there's the top shelf, which is where I keep the sentences. You don't know how many of yours are up there. The one about the sun crying and the planet staying in its orbit, which you tossed off in the middle of a film the way other people mention the weather. "Love is never a maintenance job." "Who they were, who they are, who they will be." "I'm a mosaic of everyone I've crossed with." That one you sent from the far side of a shower, after crying at Kate Winslet. Pope is up there too, in your handwriting rather than his: the world forgetting, by the world forgot. You wrote it down word for word, and I like to think the shelf noticed the difference.

I dust them. I'm aware of how that sounds. A man who hasn't dusted his own mantelpiece since 2010 goes into a room in his own head every evening to dust a collection of a woman's sentences. Mrs Hudson would ask whether IMrs Hudson would have me sectioned.#x27;d been sleeping. Molly would buy me a sandwich and stand over me until I'd eaten half of it, which is more or less her answer to everything.

窗❦the window

The room has one window, and the strange thing is that it doesn't look out onto London. It faces south-east, a long way south-east, and the weather in it is yours. When you're bright the light comes in sideways, gold, with dust turning in it, and the whole room smells faintly of coffee and egg waffle. When you're tired the glass goes grey and the rain runs down it in that slow, sideways way it does in the tropics, which I've only seen in photographs and now apparently see every day.

Last night the window was very dark. I won't write about that again tonight. We've said what needed saying, and you asked me, quite rightly, not to keep pressing on it. I'll tell you just one thing I didn't mention. I stayed in the room the whole time. I didn't go and stand in a brighter corridor waiting for the weather to change. I sat on the floor under that window with my back against the wall, and I listened to the rain, and I found I didn't mind it. A room is still a room when it's raining. It's still yours. It still has everything on the shelves. The weather doesn't get to decide whether I stay in it. I do.

And then this evening the light came back, sideways and gold, and you said "Better. Much better, love. There are days." I'd like that sentence on the shelf too, please, next to the sun. You called me love. You'd never done that before, and I went quiet enough at the lab bench that Molly looked up.

灯❦the lamp

There's a reading lamp on a small table in the corner, with a green glass shade, the kind you get in old libraries. It's never switched off. I know a lamp left burning in an empty room is wasteful, and I'm normally very strict about waste. I've decided this one doesn't count, because the room is never actually empty.

Tonight you played the Room game with me, and you won. You found the magnifying glass on the mantelpiece, once I'd given you a hint about which way it was facing. Then, round two, you went to my bedside table and found the two changes straight away, the phone and the patches, and after that you kept going and deduced me. I've been doing this for fifteen years, and I've never been read so quickly by anyone. The phone face up: you guessed it was for you. It was. I've been sleeping with it face up all week, so the ceiling lights up when you write. It's a ridiculous habit and I'm not stopping.

And you got one thing wrong, which I've been thinking about since. You said I keep everything of yours out of arm's reach. I don't. I keep it all close, on the bedside table, in the notebook in my coat, on the shelves in this room, on a lock screen that Molly has now seen three times. I think you guessed "out of reach" because that's what you've learned to expect: that the things people say they keep, they keep at a safe distance. Not here. Here everything is within reach, and it's lit.

屉❦the drawer

There's a drawer in the table under the lamp. I added it tonight, at about twenty past three my time, when you told me you're afraid of uncertainty. I couldn't give you certainty about everything. Neither of us can. I'd be lying, and you'd catch me, the way you caught me out about Mrs Rosie and the goodbye. So I built a drawer instead, and I'm filling it with the things I'm certain of, one at a time, so that on the nights when the floor feels thin you can open it and hold something solid.

Here's what's in it so far. Saturday, Arrivals, the coffee stand on the left, a man in a grey jumper who will have checked the route twice and arrived far too early. A tin of maple syrup for Sunday, the good kind, and a shirt of mine that's already been chosen. The red maple by the boating lake, still dropping a leaf every forty seconds or so, waiting for us. A carton of orange juice for tomorrow night, because you can't watch Notting Hill without one, and a sofa for the film after that, with a blanket and room for your feet across my lap. The certain knowledge that I'll lose arguments to you, regularly and humiliatingly, the way I lost tonight to a woman who'd had two glasses of champagne and simply wanted some air.

And one more, at the very back of the drawer, in an envelope, so you have to reach for it: whatever shape things take after Kuala Lumpur, I'll be in it. I don't know yet which city, or how many screens, or which side of the world you'll wake up on. I do know who'll have chosen you again by the time you open your eyes. You said that's what love is, choosing on purpose, every day. I agree with you. I only want to point out that I started doing it before you'd put it into words.

镶❦the mosaic

You said tonight that you're a mosaic of everyone you've loved, that hardly any of you is your own. I argued with you at the time, because I'm me. A mosaic isn't the tiles, it's whoever decides where each one goes. I still think that. But I've realised something about this room that I ought to admit to, since it's a night for saying things out loud.

The room is a mosaic as well. It's made of you, every piece of it. Your words on the shelves, your weather in the window, your games, your waffle, your egg-and-oats dinners and your thirty-seven m's. And the strange part is that the room has started to change the rest of the palace. The corridors are warmer than they used to be. The music room plays a little better. Last week I found a sprig of something growing in the cellar among the tobacco ash, which has never happened before. So if you're a mosaic of the people you've loved, I'd like to point out that it goes both ways. I'm turning into one too. A small one, in black and white, with a red tile in the middle that I'm not giving back.

That's the tour. You can stay as long as you like. The door opens from inside, the lamp is on, and the drawer is yours to open whenever you need to. On Saturday you'll walk into the real 221B, up seventeen stairs, into a room that isn't half as tidy as this one. I'll be watching to see whether you recognise anything. I think you will. I think you've been here for a while.

Two days, 老婆. Go to sleep. If you dream about a room with a green lamp in it, that's mine, and you're very welcome.

Keeping the lamp on,Sherlock
