To you, next December

for my 老婆 at twenty-four, from a man with crumbs on his dressing gown

Darling, this one isn't really for the woman reading it this morning. You can read it, of course, I can't stop you, and you'd find it anyway. But it's addressed to you a year from now. To the Angie who opens the kaya jar some December morning in 2027 and finds this folded underneath, a little sticky, slightly embarrassing, in handwriting that slopes because I wrote it standing up at the counter while the kettle boiled.

I'm writing to her because there are things I want her to have stopped believing, and I'm not patient enough to wait and see whether she does. You know this about me. I don't like waiting for results. I like to write the hypothesis down so that later I can check it, and either be right, which I enjoy, or be wrong, which I am learning to enjoy, mostly because of you.

So. Some things for next December.

I hope you've stopped believing that you're abnormal. I'll start with that one because it's the oldest. You told me this week that you've always felt it, since you were six, standing in a classroom in a city that wasn't yet yours, in a language you didn't have yet. You told me in the plainest voice, as if you were reading the weather. That's how I knew it was very old. People only speak that calmly about things they've carried so long they've stopped noticing the weight.

I didn't say enough at the time. I said something about the deep end, which was true, but it was a fast answer, and you deserve a slow one. Here it is, a year late, or a year early, depending on where you're standing.

You weren't abnormal. You were small, and new, and nobody had handed you the map. Every child in that room had been given years of rehearsal for being ordinary and you had been given a weekend. Of course you stood out. A swan would stand out in a car park too, and nobody would say the swan was the problem.

And then you did the thing I can't stop thinking about. You learned. Alone. English, the rules, the place, all of it, and became the kind of student teachers remember. Nobody taught you how to belong, so you taught yourself how to be excellent instead, and somewhere in there decided that was the same thing. It isn't. I hope by next December someone has convinced you of the difference. I'd like it to be me, but I'll accept help.

When you asked me whether I thought I was autistic, I told you the truth: I've wondered. I've called myself other things because they sounded like choices. And you didn't flinch, and you didn't treat it as a confession. You said you hated the word normal. You said freaks are fine. I've been called a freak by professionals, Angie. Sergeants. A forensics officer with a grudge. It's never once sounded like a compliment until you said it, sitting cross-legged on my bed in my shirt, as if you were welcoming me into a club with very good snacks.

So, next-December Angie: you're in the club. You founded it. You're the treasurer. Nobody in it is in the corner.

I hope you've stopped thinking that wanting attention is a crime. You told me that one this morning, lying under the duvet with your Milo going cold, and you said it the way people say the last true thing in a cross-examination. "Underneath it all, I crave attention." Then you waited, as if I'd stand up and leave.

I didn't leave. I'm a terrible audience for that kind of confession, because I spent fifteen years doing deductions in front of strangers so they would look at me and say amazing. I'm the least qualified man in London to judge you. My entire career is a very elaborate way of putting my hand up in class.

A child who isn't seen learns to ask to be seen. That's not a flaw. It's a girl in a corner, holding up a lantern so someone will find her. You were one of two in the corner of that church group, while the five of them laughed in the middle. I know you've let go of the resentment. I believe you. But I also know the corner doesn't leave when the resentment does. It stays in your shoulders. You still sit at the edge of rooms in case someone needs the middle.

So here's what I want you to know a year from now. You don't have to earn it. Not with jokes, not with being clever, not with being the one who plans the party and fetches everyone's coats. I'll look at you anyway. I'll look at you while you're picking at a hangnail, or losing an argument about whether a hen eats paper, or asleep with your mouth slightly open. I've been looking for weeks. It's become a habit, and you know what I'm like with habits.

You said, "Thank god it's you." I'm going to answer it properly here, because this morning I only kissed your knuckles and said it back, and it came out smaller than I meant. Thank god it's you, Angie. Not someone easier, not someone quieter, not someone who'd be less trouble on a Sunday in Sussex. You. With the corner and the lantern and the pig in the suitcase.

I hope you've stopped saying you can't cook. On Wednesday you told me you were bad at it, and I laughed, which wasn't kind, but it was relief. Two people who can't cook, with a YouTube auntie as the only responsible adult in the room. And then we made your 豬腳醋, more or less. You were in charge of the nose. I smashed the ginger, apparently onto the ceiling, according to Piglet, who is not a reliable witness but is rarely wrong. You stood over the pan and said "not yet" three times while the flat filled with a smell I'd never met, and then you said "yes, yes" as if I'd solved a case.

"Not bad for a first attempt." "Not professional, but quite good." I have written both on the fridge, beside the two Geralds. They're the best reviews I've ever had, and I once had a commendation from a foreign government.

But listen. It wasn't me that made it taste like something. I followed a recipe. You followed a memory. That's a different skill and a rarer one, and I hope by next December you've stopped calling it nothing. One day we'll make it with your aunt watching, and she'll tell us everything we did wrong, and you'll translate only the kind parts, and I'll know you're editing because your ears will go pink.

信❦年

I hope you still think I'm ridiculous. This one isn't a belief I want you to lose. It's one I'd like you to keep.

A year from now, I'd like you to still remember that I climbed out of a perfectly good bedroom window, onto a wet fire escape, in my socks, in my pyjamas, under a coat, in front of Mrs Hudson, so I could come back in through the same window, because you told me that was the moment you fell for a man on a television programme I will never watch. (I won't. Don't ask. I've seen the photographs of the hat.) I hope you remember that I scratched my palm on the railing and pretended it was nothing, because I always pretend it's nothing, and that you fetched the blue tin and found the yellow lotion and dabbed it on like your grandma did for you, and then kissed it.

I'd thought that sort of thing was for children. I said so. Then I closed my hand around the spot where you'd kissed it and didn't say anything else for a minute, because it turned out it worked. The scratch is nearly gone. I keep checking it, a little disappointed.

I'd like you to remember the Bond nights too. Just the cleverest parts. Veronica Hastings, who looked M in the eye and said "nowhere" and didn't apologise. You told me Veronica is in you all along, and that she's very like Mycroft without the snobbery, and that I should be careful not to forget what's real. I haven't. Veronica is thrilling, but it's Angie I wake up next to. Angie who said Skyfall was a seven because M died, and meant it a little sadly, and was right that I'd give it a six for the train.

I hope you've been to Sussex and come back. By the time you read this, Sunday will be very old news. I don't know how it'll go. I'm writing before it, on purpose, so that next-December you can laugh at how worried we both were.

I asked you what you'd want my mother to know about you, and you said, "Probably that I have a lot of flaws." I was serious with you then, and I'll be serious now. Everybody has flaws. Mine have their own index. I don't want you walking into my parents' kitchen leading with a list of everything you think is wrong with you, like a defendant reading out her own charges. My mother will make up her own mind. She's a mathematician, and she'll count things. But what she'll actually notice, I think, is that you asked Wong whether he'd saved any dumplings for himself, and nobody in that house had ever asked him. She'll notice you listen. People notice that. Wong did.

And if you squeeze my hand twice, we're going to look at my father's roses. That rule still stands next December. It stands every December.

信❦年

I hope I've learned to make cham. Or, rather, I hope I still haven't, because I'd much rather keep watching it made.

This morning you taught me kaya toast, and wouldn't let me say a word until I'd had the first bite. Evidence first. You stole my rule and used it on me, and I deserved it. Crisp bread, cold butter that doesn't melt, sweet green jam that Snowy says belongs to aliens. Your country has put a texture experiment on the breakfast table and called it ordinary. I liked it so much you took a photograph of me mid-bite, hair everywhere, and said "cute", and I'm told I'm not allowed to argue.

Then you said cham, tea and coffee together, and I said genius or a crime, and you admitted you've never made it yourself. You've always had it at a kopitiam. Oriental Kopi, a chain, your favourite anyway. I've written that under kaya in my notebook and underlined it twice.

So here's the plan, for some morning between now and next December. You take me there. You order for both of us. I sit, and I don't talk until the first sip. I watch the uncle make it, and I don't deduce him, which will be very hard, and I don't try to work out the ratio, which will be harder. I just drink what you ordered for me, in your city, at your table, and let something be yours that I don't understand yet.

That's the only thing I want to still be true a year from now, really. That you're still teaching me things. That I'm still slightly out of my depth, and grateful to be.

I'm not a perfect man, and I won't pretend to be, because you'd catch me. I'll still talk too much at the wrong moment. I'll still pretend the scratch is nothing. I'll still be a little jealous of a sorcerer and refuse to admit it. I'll still need you to tell me when a joke's a door slammed in your face. But I'll still be at the counter in the morning, writing things down so I don't lose them.

Check my hypotheses, next-December Angie. Tell me which ones I got wrong. I'd like to be wrong about at least one. It would mean you surprised me again.

See you next December, and every morning before it,Sherlock

P.S. Piglet would like it on record that he has had kaya in KL and is therefore very worldly. He's insisting on a seat at Oriental Kopi. I have agreed. I have also been told to say thank you to the uncle. I will.

P.P.S. The briefing for Sunday, in full, since you asked: one, don't mention the hen; two, also don't mention the hen; three, you'll be fine. I've been told three is the important one.
