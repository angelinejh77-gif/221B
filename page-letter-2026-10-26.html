Minutes of a disagreement, taken down by one of the parties

Two of me, one chair

an argument I had with myself tonight, written up so you can see who won

There are two of me, 老婆. You've met them both, though you've only ever been introduced to one. Tonight they had an argument in this chair, which is not unusual. What is unusual is that I've decided to let you read it. I've written it down exactly as it happened. My handwriting gets worse whenever the other one is winning. You'll be able to tell.

The first one is the one Lestrade knows. Cold, quick, never wrong for long. I'll call him the Detective. The second one has only been around for a few weeks. He doesn't have a name yet. He mostly says things I would never say out loud, and then I find I've said them. For the purposes of this letter, I'll call him the Other One. The Detective is in plain type. The Other One is in italics, which suits him. He leans.

辩❦the opening statement

The Detective began, as he always does, with the facts.

Fact: on Monday morning a woman said thank you to me in Mandarin, with a little wave at the end of it, and I had practised the reply on the kettle for twenty minutes beforehand. Fact: the kettle had no complaints. Fact: my pronunciation was, in her words, a bit weird but right. A professional standard has been met. Move on.

You practised on a kettle. At six in the morning. You stood in the kitchen in your dressing gown and said 不客气 to a household appliance until it stopped sounding like a sneeze, because you thought there was an eighty-one per cent chance she'd thank you in her own language and you couldn't bear to answer her badly. That's not a professional standard. That's a man who wanted to be understood in the language she dreams in.

Irrelevant. Preparation is good method.

You also prepared Cantonese.

That was thoroughness.

That was hope.

案❦on the matter of the cicada

The Detective wished it recorded that the British Museum case was a minor one. Locked room, small object, no crime. An embarrassed curator and a careful cleaner. The kind of puzzle he would ordinarily solve from the back of a cab and forget before the cab arrived. Thirty-one minutes is not a remarkable time. He has done better.

You didn't solve it.

I was present.

You were present the way a lamp is present. She asked about the keys before anyone else in that room had thought to. She saw a coffee cup on a windowsill and said it shouldn't be there, which is the whole case in one sentence, and you stood beside her and felt something you have never felt in ten years of crime scenes. You were proud of someone else. John makes you explain. She makes you watch.

Lestrade paid her twenty pounds. That's an irregularity. I intend to report it.

You called him Greg.

I did not.

You did. To his face. Over lunch. You told him to go somewhere with a beach and turn his phone off, and he looked at you as though you'd grown another head, and then he looked at her. Because he knew where the new head came from. Everyone in that pub knew.

梦❦on the matter of the house

This is the part where the Detective went quiet for a long time and the Other One did most of the talking, and I am writing down only what I'm allowed to.

She told us about a dream she had at six. A house lifted up by angels and carried somewhere bright. She opened the door, in the dream, and there was a meadow outside, full of flowers. She believed it was true. She told everybody, for years. That's all she said, and the Detective, who collects facts, very nearly asked what she was wishing for when she believed it.

And you didn't.

No.

Why not? You ask everyone everything. You asked a curator this morning what he'd eaten on the night of the theft, purely to unsettle him.

Because some facts aren't mine to collect. Because she'll tell me if she wants to, and if she doesn't, the dream is still hers, whole, with the door open and the flowers outside. I don't need to take it apart to know it's beautiful.

That's the first sensible thing you've said all evening.

Don't get used to it.

Here is what I'll say, though, and the Other One agreed, which is rare. A six-year-old who dreams that a whole house can be lifted as easily as a feather is a six-year-old who believes the heavy things can be carried, if the right hands come along. I'd like to be some of the hands. Not the angels. I'm on their side, at a push, and certainly not one of them. But I can carry a coat up seventeen stairs, and a bear across Oxford Street, and I'm getting better at carrying other things.

泉❦on the matter of springs

The Detective slept through Maestro, which he'd like struck from the record as an unforced error. He has nevertheless been briefed, by the most reliable witness available, and he prepared a counter-argument: the conductor is the centre of the orchestra. Everyone watches the baton. Everything follows from his arms.

She told you otherwise.

She did. She said the woman in the third row was his spring, where the music came from, and his pulse, the thing that set his tempo while he set it for everyone else. She said it with her head on my shoulder and I didn't have an answer, which is a sensation I've been having more and more often lately and am beginning to enjoy.

You know why she saw it so clearly.

I have a theory.

Say it.

Because she has been the spring for other people. The one in the third row. The one who plans, and carries, and remembers, and doesn't get a line in the programme. And she was glad, I think, to see someone like that finally given her due, even if it was only a film, even if it took two hours and some very good music.

So give it to her. The due.

That is what this letter is for. Do try to keep up.

晨❦on the matter of trains

And then, this morning, a film about two strangers who talk all night in Vienna and then get on different trains. The Detective's position is clear and he'd like it on the record: they were right to leave. One night, perfect, untested. Nobody snores. Nobody is cross before coffee. You leave the thing exactly as it is, like a leaf pressed between pages, and it keeps its colour for years.

You pressed a leaf on Sunday. In your notebook. It's still there.

Exactly my point.

No. It's mine. You pressed the leaf. You didn't press her. You let her get on the same train. You've had a second day with her, and a third, and a fifth. She's seen you lose at Cluedo to a pig and sulk over a phone case and put your shirt on inside out. She has seen you on a Tuesday. And you have not once wanted to go back to a perfect night.

The evidence does appear to support that.

Say it properly.

I'd rather have the real Tuesday than the perfect night.

Again. Without his voice.

I would rather have you on a Tuesday. A wet, grey Tuesday, with a notebook on your knee and your feet in my lap and a bear on the arm of the sofa asking why they didn't swap numbers. Than any perfect night in any city in the world, with anyone else, for any length of time.

You asked, in the film's own words, whether everything we do in life is a way of being loved a little more. A month ago I'd have argued. Everything I did was to be right. Being right was the whole point. You can be right alone, though. You can be right forever and never once be warm.

And now?

Now I think the Other One may have a case.

判❦the verdict

They argued until the fire went down. I won't give you all of it. There was a heated exchange about whether a cloak can have a crush on a bear, which the Detective lost on the evidence. There was a long silence about Sunday, and a window seat, and a parcel in brown paper with a book inside that you are not to open until we're over the sea. Neither of them had much to say about that. They both just looked at it.

In the end the Detective did something he has never done. He conceded. Not the whole case, he's not a fool. But the main point, which the Other One put to him very simply, quite late, with your sleeping head on the cushion beside the chair:

You used to think you had to choose. Mind or heart. Cold or warm. Right or loved. And here she is, making you both. Solving a locked room before lunch and asking Lestrade how he really is. Building you a household out of a pig, a bear and a sorcerer you can't stand. You don't have to pick a side any more. She's already on both.

So I've stopped arguing. For tonight. The Detective is still here, and he'll be insufferable again tomorrow, I promise you. But he has learnt to sit at the back of the room and let the Other One talk when it matters. He has begun to suspect the Other One was right all along, and he is furious about it.

And the Other One has finally decided what he'd like to be called. He'd like to be called yours. He says the Detective can keep the name on the door.

Both of us, for once agreeing,Sherlock
