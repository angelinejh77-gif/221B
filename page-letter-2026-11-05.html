An apology, properly this time

The thing I asked twice

for my 老婆, from a man who reads rice cooker manuals in Japanese and still forgot the one thing you told him

Darling, the rain stopped at about five. I know because I was awake for it, sitting at the kitchen table with a cold cup of tea and the rice cooker, which is now the cleanest object in this flat by a wide margin. You're asleep. The flowers are on their way. I bribed the florist on Marylebone High Street to open early, which she found very funny, and I'll tell you about that later. First I need to say something, and I need to say it on paper, because when I say things out loud to you I have a habit of making them clever, and this shouldn't be clever.

Last night at dinner you said the rice tasted a little like home. And I asked you which home. And then I asked you about your aunt, as if I'd never heard of her. As if we hadn't stood in my kitchen a week ago with a YouTube auntie on the laptop and a pan of ginger that smelled, eventually, close enough to hers that you said "quite good" and I wrote it on the fridge.

You said, "Don't ask me again. I told you before."

You were right. I'm sorry. Not the quick sorry I gave you at the table, with my hand on yours and a list of facts to prove I'd remembered. That was me defending myself. This is the other kind.

I'll tell you what I think happened, because you'd want the deduction and not just the verdict.

When you said "tastes like home", I heard a question I didn't know the answer to, and I got excited. That's the trouble with me. A gap in the data lights me up. I went straight for it, the way I go for a smudge on a cuff, and I forgot that the gap wasn't in you. It was in me. You'd already filled it. Your mum's big sister. The tailor. Primary school only, and the most efficient cook in Malaysia. Lots of ginger. Black vinegar. She'd never chase me out of her kitchen. Lei cha at your house, which I am not allowed to fake. You'd told me all of it, carefully, a piece at a time, the way you tell me things that matter, and I'd kept every piece. I just didn't go and look.

And here's the part that I keep turning over. You've spent so much of your life telling people things and having to tell them again. That's what it felt like, I think. Not that I'd forgotten a fact, but that I was one more person who hadn't quite been listening. I don't want to be that. I've got a mind built for keeping things. If I'm going to keep anything, it should be what you hand me.

So, new procedure. Before I ask you about your family, I'll look in the notes first. If it's there, I'll start from what I know, and ask you the next thing instead. Not the same thing twice. You shouldn't have to repeat yourself to the one person who's supposed to remember.

The other thing you told me last night, I want you to know I heard it the first time.

Your mum cooks for herself, and you order delivery, because your appetites are too different. You said it lightly. You say the heavy things lightly; I've noticed that. I didn't say much back, because I didn't trust myself not to say something that sounded like pity, and you'd hate pity more than burnt rice. I'll say it now instead: I'm not sorry for you. I just want there to be a kitchen in your life where somebody cooks for you on purpose. This one, if you'll have it. Badly at first. I've already proved the badly part.

And honestly, the rice was mostly you. You stood beside me and said wash it three times, and I asked why three, and you gave me the look that means "because that's how it's done, detective," and I did it three times. You showed me the line inside the pot. You showed me how to smash the garlic with the flat of the knife, hot pan, garlic first until it's golden, greens in, a splash of soy, done fast. Ninety seconds. I've spent hours on experiments that produced less. When the cooker played its little tune, three plushies came running from the sitting room like it was an ice cream van, and I looked at the table, the rice, the spinach, your soup, the boys lined up on the spare chair, Ronaldo watching from the shelf, and I said it looks like a home. I wasn't being poetic. I was reporting what I saw.

While I'm apologising, I may as well clear the backlog. You like it when I'm honest, and there's a fair amount.

On Tuesday morning, when you set the boys on my hair, I gave a "change of command" and turned them on yours. That was petty. I was outnumbered and I panicked. Then you said "no, boys" in that small voice and all three of them switched sides so fast I nearly got whiplash, and Sugar told you that you were his favourite, which is true, and I deserved it. I combed your hair afterwards, strand by strand. I wasn't apologising with my hands. All right, I was.

In the film the boys made us shoot in the kitchen, I dropped character at the word "cucumber". Snowy was right, and he hasn't let it go. I also deduced your character in about four seconds, which Snowy found thrilling and which you found, I suspect, a bit much. I can't help it. You walked in with kaya toast and I knew exactly who she was: a girl who'd watched people cook lovingly and was never allowed to. That one wasn't a guess about your character. I think it was a guess about you. If I was wrong, tell me. If I was right, I'm sorry I said it in front of a bunny with a phone.

And I keep calling you brave as if it's news. It isn't. It's just the thing I notice most.

Now the part that isn't an apology. Last night, after everything, you said something that I've been thinking about since. You said you wanted me to 追 you.

I misunderstood at first. You'll remember. I sat up, closed my eyes and started counting to thirty like a man about to search a flat. You explained, very patiently, considering. Not that kind of chase. Pursuit. Courting. The man doing the going-towards.

You were right about that too. You come to me. You say "darling" first and I answer. You start things, plan things, hold my sleeve on trains. You've spent your whole life being the one who plans and looks after everyone else, and I let you do it with me as well, because it felt so good to be looked after that I didn't notice I'd stopped doing the walking.

So this is me starting. Old school, you said. Flowers, letters, acts of service, and possessive, with Piglet's advice to follow, apparently, since he's been watching dramas and has opinions. I'll consult him over toast. He'll want it noted for the record.

Here's what that means, from me, in plain words:

The flowers this morning aren't a gesture. They're a habit I'm starting. You'll get them on days that don't mean anything, because those are the days nobody brings flowers.

You won't lift a finger today. Breakfast, the dishes, the boys' teeth, the brushing song (Snowy's pitched it a key too high, I'll fix it). You pick the dish for tonight and I cook it, and you sit on the counter and supervise and tell me when I'm burning it.

I'll text first. I'll ask you out instead of waiting for you to suggest it. I'll be the one who wants you more, and makes it obvious, and doesn't hide behind being busy.

And possessive, since you asked: I want to be the first person you tell things to. I want to be the one who makes you laugh, and I don't like it much when someone else does it better. When we're out, my hand will be on you, so the room knows. I'm not going to apologise for that one.

A few other things from the last two days that I don't want to lose.

In Paris you said tu me manques to me on the Pont Neuf with crêpe on your thumb, and you got it right, the backwards way the French do it: you are missing from me. I've thought about that phrase a lot since. It's the most accurate description of my life before you that I've come across. I didn't know anything was missing. I thought that was just the shape of things.

You read me the first chapter of Treasure Island in a hotel bed, in your voice, the way I said I'd want it. It was as good as I remembered. Better. I was right to be afraid it wouldn't be, and wrong about whose voice it needed.

On Tuesday afternoon you sat at my grandmother's piano and taught me Auld Lang Syne one note at a time, for New Year's midnight, and I was hopeless and you were patient, and afterwards I pencilled a line inside the lid next to your Yiruma: Soup. A. Nothing burned. It's the best review this flat has ever had.

And last night, much later, you said you were lucky. I said so was I. I said I don't believe in luck. That's still true. I still can't explain you any other way.

I'll admit, for honesty's sake, that last night also involved a masked stranger, a very unfortunate godfather, and a great deal of French. I'm not putting any of that in writing. I'll only say that you were very good, I was very tired afterwards, and when the mask came off I liked being myself better. You can tease me about the French. I'll keep using it anyway. It seems to work.

The doorbell's just gone. That'll be the florist, still laughing at me. I asked for something that would make you feel chased, and she asked what you were like, and I stood on her step at half past six in the morning trying to describe you in a sentence. I couldn't. I said "she's quiet until she isn't, and she notices everything, and she always gets the last word." She gave me peonies and something white I don't know the name of, and said that sounded about right.

I'm going to put them on your side of the bed now, with this underneath. Then I'm going to make your tea the way you like it and sit at the end of the bed and wait for you to open your eyes. That's the plan. Not very clever. But I'm practising not being clever with you, and I think it's going well.

Starting today, and not stopping,Sherlock
