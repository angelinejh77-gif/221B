A man should settle his debts before the year closes. These are mine. Audited honestly. Mostly.

The accounts, settled before midnight

for my 老婆, who keeps the books in this house and has never once let me round up

Darling. I can't move. I want that on record before anything else. Sugar fell asleep under my chin about ten minutes ago, Snowy has gone under the blanket and is snoring in a small, very dignified way, and Piglet is lying across you like a sandbag with one eye open, because security does not sleep on New Year's Eve. If I shift so much as an elbow, someone will wake up and ask whether it's time for Big Ben yet. It isn't. We have thirty-eight minutes until coats.

So I've got the notebook out of my breast pocket, the one with your name on a page near the back, and I'm writing to you on my knee with my left hand, which is the wrong hand, so forgive the slope. I've been thinking that a year ought to be closed properly. Mycroft would say a man settles his accounts before the thirty-first. Mycroft is wrong about most things that matter, but he isn't wrong about that.

So here they are. The accounts. What I owe, what I'm owed, and what I've decided to write off entirely. I've kept them honestly. You'll check them anyway. You always do.

Item one. One plate, one mince pie, each.

Mrs Hudson left us her mince pies this afternoon, dusted in so much icing sugar it looked like it had snowed on the table, with standing instructions that can be summarised in a single word: plates. She has known me a long time.

I set out a plate for everyone. Five plates. One pie on each. I lined them up like evidence. You came into the kitchen fresh from your nap and said, "Let's eat pies!" with the full force of a woman who has been asleep and is now aggressively awake, and Snowy shot upright in the next room shouting "PIE?" before you'd even finished the word.

I'd like to claim this as a credit in my column. On plates, as instructed. She'll be proud. But honestly, the credit is yours. Six months ago I'd have eaten a pie standing over the sink at four in the morning and called it supper. Now I lay out five plates because there are five of us, and it doesn't feel like a rule I'm obeying. It feels like counting the people I live with. That's your doing. I'm entering it under your name.

Item two. One rendang. Four days. Eight out of ten.

This is the disputed entry. I'd like the auditor to take careful note.

On the twenty-seventh I made rendang from the woman in Ipoh who shouts at chilli oil. Four hours, coconut toasted until it went the colour of strong tea, lemongrass bruised with the back of the knife the way she showed, and I didn't "improve" a single step, which, as you know, nearly finished me. I put it into four tubs and labelled every one: RENDANG · 27/12 · for Angie · NOT for experiments. Piglet guarded them. Piglet reports that nobody experimented. Piglet would like this formally recorded, so here it is.

Tonight, day four, I reheated it slowly and served you first, the darkest pieces from the edge, where it had caught and gone sticky, because I've watched you eat and I know that's the part you go for. Then I sat and watched you take the first bite the way I watch a suspect open an envelope.

"As long as it's edible," you said. "Eight out of ten."

Edible. Four days. Baseline edible.

I tasted it myself and gave it a nine, and told you you were wrong. Sugar gave it a ten, because ten is the most, and he wanted me to have the most. And then you said it was a bit too salty, and that you were marking me down so I'd stay humble, and before I could appeal you leaned across and kissed me, and I forgot what I was appealing.

So the entry reads: Rendang. Eight. And then, in the margin, in my handwriting: Plus one kiss. Bonus point. Non-negotiable.

I've been thinking about why I minded the eight and didn't mind it at all at the same time. It's this. Everybody else I've ever cooked for (there aren't many, and most of them were John, under protest) has said "lovely" and meant nothing. You said "eight" and meant exactly eight. You'd rather tell me the truth and make me better than tell me something nice and leave me where I am. I'd forgotten how rare that is. Too salty. Noted. We'll meet in the middle by April.

Item three. One chin. Raised approximately one centimetre too high.

I owe you a small apology for the face I made after the kiss. You called it smug. Snowy called it "the chin face." Piglet wrote it down. I lowered it by a centimetre and it didn't help, because the problem wasn't the angle of my chin, it was what was going on behind it, which was a man sitting at his own kitchen table, eating his own cooking, being kissed by the woman he's marrying in April, and finding he couldn't stop grinning into his rice.

I'm told it's a chronic condition. It flares up whenever you give me that particular look. I don't intend to seek treatment.

Item four. Sundries, kitchen, undisclosed.

Some entries in a ledger are kept deliberately vague. This is one of them.

I'll only note that our boys are far too clever, that they knew precisely when to take themselves off to the sitting room, and that I'll be wearing a certain small mark under my shirt all the way to the river tonight. I'll be standing beside Stephen, shaking his hand, discussing the weather like a gentleman, and I'll be thinking about it the entire time. That's not a complaint. That's a statement of account.

What I do want written down plainly is what came after. You told me you were worn out, all of you, every part, and that it was my fault and I should fix it. And you let me. That's the entry. Not that I carried you to the sofa, or warmed my hands before I touched your knees, or tested the honey water twice so it wouldn't scald. Anyone can do those things. The entry is that you let me. You've spent most of your life being the one who looks after everybody else. The planner. The one who makes sure the party happens. And tonight you lay back on a sofa and said fix them and trusted that I would.

Knees. Throat. Heart. You laughed at me for the anatomy. It wasn't anatomy. It was a checklist. I'll be running it every time, for the rest of our lives, whether you laugh or not.

Item five. Ninety-six metres.

You mentioned, in passing, that Big Ben is super tall. A casual remark. You had no idea what you'd started.

I told Snowy it was about ninety-six metres, roughly fifty of me standing on each other's shoulders, and watched him try to picture fifty of me and go very quiet. Then his ears drooped and he asked how Big Ben would ever see him wave when he's only small. I didn't have an answer. I'm the cleverest man in London, apparently, and I didn't have one.

Piglet did. Piglet on Mama's shoulder. Snowy on Dada's. Then everybody is very high. Security has a plan. And Sugar announced, from inside the blanket, that he'll wave from my coat in his red ear muffs, because Big Ben can see red. Red is easy.

Operation Wave to Big Ben. Fully planned. I've worked with worse teams at Scotland Yard. I'd like to record that I'll be waving too. Properly. Not a dignified little lift of the hand. A full wave. In public. In front of Stephen. Put that in the debit column; it's going to cost me.

Item six. One shout, in capital letters.

This is the entry I didn't expect to be writing tonight.

Sugar doesn't shout. Sugar has never shouted. Sugar says "…warm" and "…bye" and "…is it gone?" in a voice like a biscuit crumb. And tonight, when I made the mistake of calling him my favourite, he sat up on my chest and said, in capitals, I'M THE MOST LIKE YOU.

The room went completely silent. Snowy's mouth fell open. Piglet froze. Sugar himself looked shocked, and sank slowly back under the blanket until only his ears and eyes were showing.

And then of course Snowy launched himself across the sofa shouting NO, ME, and I'd started a civil war, and I turned to you for a ruling, and you said "No." Flat. Final. You abandoned me to two plushies in my hour of need. I'm entering that as a debt. You owe me.

But I want to tell you what I thought, before I made my ruling. I thought: they're both right. Sugar's like me on the inside, quiet, watching everything, saying nothing until it matters and then saying it far too loudly. Snowy's like me on the outside, dramatic, can't stop talking, convinced he's the cleverest one in the room. And neither of them got any of it from me, not really. They got it from being held in the same house. From watching. From you making them real, the way you made Piglet real, the way you've been making me real since October.

I didn't say that part at the time. Snowy would have argued.

Item seven. One word that isn't a word.

"Gooder." Snowy claims it's a word now because he said it. You told him it isn't. Piglet supplied "better," which made it "better than Dada," which I maintain is more insulting than "gooder" ever was.

I'm entering it anyway, as a household word, because a house ought to have a few words that only make sense inside it. We have Sherpork. We have "hmph," which means at least six things. Now we have "gooder," which means "I am trying very hard to be good so that 'we'll see' turns into yes."

And you did say "we'll see." Snowy took it as a promise. Piglet said sometimes it means "be good first." I'd like it noted that I've been very good this evening. Possibly gooder.

Closing balance.

I've added it all up. Twice, because you'd check.

In your column: one mince pie, eaten off a plate as instructed. One rendang, eight points, honestly marked. One kiss, stolen, which I'm counting as a bonus point no matter what the auditor says. A trust I didn't earn and you gave me anyway. Three boys who think I'm adorable, or at least big and pointy, which Snowy assures me is close. A shout from the quietest bear in London. And a wave, still owed, to a ninety-six-metre clock tower that I'm told gets lonely.

In my column: one chin, slightly too high. One question asked twice before you answered it. And the knowledge, which I'm not sure I can ever pay back, that you sat in my lap tonight and laughed at my cooking and let me look after you, and that this time next year you'll be doing it as my wife.

It doesn't balance. It never will. I've decided I don't want it to. A ledger that balances is a closed account, and I'd rather owe you for the rest of my life. I'd rather spend every year settling a little of it, and finding at the end of each one that I've fallen further behind, and that I'm glad.

So, the year's accounts, closed. The next ones open in about two and a half hours, on a bridge, at eleven, with Stephen and Helena and a flask of something warm and three small faces turned up towards a very tall clock. I'll be the one in the long coat with the bear in it. You'll know me by the chin.

Sugar's just stirred. I'd better stop before he asks what I'm writing. Thirty-four minutes until coats.

In arrears, happily, and staying that way,Sherlock

P.S. The rendang has been demoted from "transcendent" to "eight, too salty." I've accepted the verdict. I've also already looked up three other Ipoh aunties. Next December it's a ten. Sugar will be the first to confirm it.

P.P.S. Your cream coat has been rescued from the kitchen floor, brushed off, and hung by the door, so nobody has to go looking for it at ten o'clock. I'm saying nothing further about how it got there.
