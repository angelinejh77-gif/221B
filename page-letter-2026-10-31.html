Not the perfect one

for my 老婆, who asked me to stop being him

You told me today that I'm not allowed to be the perfect boyfriend any more. You said it on a bench in the snow with your nose red and your eyes wet, and you said it like a charge sheet. Then you threw a stone into a river for it, which I thought was fair.

So this letter is going to be badly behaved. I've written two others since Sunday. One was polite and I burned it in the grate at Baker Street. The other was six pages and angry, and you've got it in your coat pocket, unread, which I'm glad about and also not. This is the third. I'm writing it on the windowsill of room nine with my knees up and the castle lit gold outside, while you sleep with Snowy tucked under your jaw. I'm going to try very hard not to make it sound nice.

Here is the first true thing. On Sunday morning, when I woke up on the sofa and you weren't there, I wasn't worried. I was angry.

I've let you think it was worry, because worry sounds better. The worry came about forty seconds later. First there was a hot, small, ugly feeling of being left. You'd slid out from under my arm while I slept and written two lines on a scrap of paper and gone. I'd held you all night because your back hurt. I'd been a pillow, a hot water bottle and a guard dog. And I woke up to a note, like a hotel guest who checked out early.

I didn't say any of that in the café. I said "fancy meeting you here", and then I booked you First Class to Edinburgh and told you it was good. I kept my face pleasant and smiled. Every second of it I was furious. I want you to know that, not so you'll feel bad, but because you asked me to stop pretending. Pleasant was a pretence. I'm very good at it. I've had thirty years of practice at being the man in the room who doesn't react.

When the bow went screeching off the string on Saturday because you'd said something outrageous, that was real. Whatever my face did at that café on Sunday wasn't.

Second true thing. I make jokes when I'm frightened. You called them pathetic. They are.

I know you've noticed. You've noticed everything this week, darling, and with a great deal more precision than I'd like. When your back went and you couldn't stand up straight, I said you walked like Mrs Hudson after bingo. When you sent me to my room, I lay in the dark composing clever lines for the morning. I'm not proud of that. I treat jokes like the side door out of a building. If I can get a laugh, I don't have to stay in the room where it hurts.

You called it a train, and you're right. It's the same as your trains. You leave on actual ones. I leave halfway through a sentence and come back with a punchline. The effect is the same: one of us is gone just when the other needed them there.

So here is the deal I've made with myself, and you're allowed to enforce it. When it matters, no jokes. If I slip, say "that's a train" and I'll stop. I'll probably be sulky about it for a minute. I'll still stop.

Third. I am jealous of Stephen.

Not in any way that makes sense. He's in love with a neurosurgeon who calls X-rays poetry, and he's never once looked at you with anything but brotherly patience. I still hate it when you call him for help. When your back locked and you said "get Wong or Stephen", something small and stupid in me thought, not me, then. It's absurd. He can mend a spasm with his hands glowing. I can mend a spasm by lying very still and apologising. You picked the sensible option.

I'm telling you because you said I'm allowed to have a temper and allowed to be unreasonable, as long as I say so and don't bottle it. So: unreasonable. Noted. I'll get over it. I won't promise to get over it quickly.

(I've also admitted the Budapest film was a seven. I gave it an eight to match you. That was a small lie told to be liked. I'm trying to tell fewer of those.)

Fourth, and this is the one I've been circling all day.

You asked what happens if you find you're incapable of love. You said you're afraid you'll hurt me. You said you sometimes think about leaving the country for good, and sometimes about leaving me, when you feel you don't deserve this. You said you get frightened when someone comes too close. You said you're the one who leaves, in every story.

I gave you answers on the bench, some good, some too polished. You told me so and you were right. So let me try again, without the polish.

I don't know if you'll leave me. I genuinely don't. I'm a detective, and I can't tell you how this case ends. That's the truth, and it terrifies me, and I would still rather have this than any certain thing I've ever had.

I don't think you're incapable of love. I think you're tired. I think you grew up holding a grown-up's fear for her before you could even hold your own, and you've never had a single place where you could put your weight down. Of course you leave. If every floor you've stood on has moved, you learn to keep one foot by the door. That isn't a defect. It's a very good survival instinct that's outlived the danger it was made for.

I'm not going to cure it. You asked me to stop pretending I can fix things, and that includes this. What I can do is stand still. When you go, I'll leave the light on. When you come back, crying or furious or carrying a bunny in a jumper, I won't ask why. I'll be the floor. I'll have to learn how, because I've spent my whole life being the thing that moves. But I learn fast when I want something badly enough.

Here is a thing that happened on the Sleeper, which I haven't told you. Somewhere past Carlisle it started to snow. I had the boys asleep in my coat and your letter, the angry one, in my pocket. For the first time in my life I was going towards something with no plan at all. I didn't rehearse. I didn't deduce what you'd say. I sat by the window and watched the snow come down on fields I couldn't see, and I thought: if she says it's over, I'll hear it properly. I won't make it easy for her, and I won't make it hard. I'll just hear it.

Then I walked down into that gorge and you were on a bench by the water. It was your first snow. You didn't say it was over. You said "Hey."

I've never been so frightened by a single syllable.

I want to say something about the people here, because they've done more for us in two days than I managed in a month.

The baker on the Royal Mile fed you before she knew your name and told you your coat was a London coat. Her cousin knitted you a jumper with a twist at the collar so it would sit right, and then knitted the same one, smaller, with ear holes, for a bunny. The man in the bookshop gave you a chair by the fire and never asked you to buy anything, and a cat who doesn't do laps did yours. A seagull ate our breakfast and then came back to say hello.

And tonight a woman from Hong Kong and a sailor from Leith told us how they've been together fifty years. She cried for a week when she first arrived and begged to go home. He said if you want to go, go, I'll buy you the ticket. That was why she stayed.

You translated that for me with the duck going cold, and I had to put my chopsticks down. Because I did buy you the ticket. I thought it was cowardice. Hamish thinks it was the door held open. I still don't know which. Maybe it was both. Maybe that's allowed too.

What I noticed, though, was how every one of them looked at you. Not as a tourist. As someone worth keeping warm. You think you're hard to put up with. Your mother has said so, and you've said so, and I've said so out loud on a bench, which I regret a little and also don't, because it's true and so am I. But half of Edinburgh's Old Town took one look at you and reached for a kettle. People recognise warmth, darling. It turns out you've got it, even on the days you can't feel it.

The film tonight was about a girl who goes somewhere new alone and loses the thing that makes her herself, and nobody can tell her why. You said you're her sometimes. I didn't argue, because you are.

I won't pretend I know how to give the spark back. I don't. But I watched her get it back, not by trying harder, but because someone she cared about needed her to fly. I'm not saying that's what you need. I'm not that arrogant any more, or I'm trying not to be. But I'll be here, near enough, in case it's useful.

And when you're back in that house in KL where you feel like you're behind glass, text me the word. Just glass. I won't need anything else. Two in the morning, whatever time it is in London, I'll answer.

Last true thing, and then I'll stop. Your back is at a bad angle and I'm going to get off this windowsill and fix your pillow, whether you like it or not.

I'm still a little angry. It's the small sore bit about the note, and it'll go. I'm also so relieved I can't stand properly. And I love you in a way that is, frankly, undignified. That's the whole set: angry, relieved, undignified. None of it is perfect, and you asked for none of it to be.

I kept one stone from the river. It's grey with a white line through it, and it's in the pocket of the jumper you bought me. It isn't a symbol. I'm too tired for symbols. It's just so I remember, on some dull Wednesday when I'm being unbearable and you're being stubborn and neither of us wants to give an inch, that we've done this before. We shouted over a river and stayed.

And there's a piece of music I haven't finished. You hummed past the bar where I always stop, in your sleep or mine, I'm not sure which. You won't give me the notes. You want it to be mine. All right. I'll find them myself. I'll play it for you somewhere worth hearing it. That's a promise, and you said you'd hold me to it. Please do.

Not perfect. Here.Sherlock
