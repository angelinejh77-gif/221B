A plan, made carefully and then abandoned

What I'd show you first

the first five minutes after Arrivals, rehearsed in great detail by a man who already knows he won't follow any of it

If you're reading this, the wheels are down. You've been in a metal tube for fourteen hours with a baby two rows away, a pig in your backpack and a WiFi signal that came and went like a bad informant, and now you're somewhere on the western edge of London with the seatbelt light still on, reading a letter I wrote while you were over the sea. I hope it's warmer where you're sitting than it is outside. It won't be warmer outside. That's the first thing I'd like you to know about my city, and the last unkind thing I'll say about it tonight.

I'm writing at the desk by the window. It's the middle of the afternoon here. The light on Baker Street has gone the colour of weak tea, and for about the last hour I've been doing something I'm not proud of. I've been planning the first five minutes after you walk through those doors. Not roughly. In detail, with timings, the way I'd plan an arrest. I'd like to show you the plan, because I think you'll enjoy it, and because I've already decided to throw it away. You might as well see what I'm throwing away.

门❦minute one

The doors at Arrivals don't open the way people imagine. They slide apart in two halves, there's a little lip at the bottom that catches suitcase wheels, and everyone comes through looking exactly the same: tired, slightly startled, searching. I've watched them. I went on Wednesday, without telling John, and stood at the barrier for forty minutes watching a flight from Singapore come in. A woman in a security vest asked if I was all right. I said I was conducting research. She said, "On what?" and I discovered I didn't have an answer I was willing to say out loud.

What I learned is this: people coming through those doors don't see anyone for the first three seconds. Their eyes go straight to the far wall, as if the building might have instructions for them. Then they start looking at faces. So the plan for minute one was to stand completely still, on the left, by the coffee stand, and let you find me. Not wave. Not call out. Just be the one fixed point in a moving room, and let you work it out. You're good at that. You worked out Angier's limp an hour before the film admitted it.

I'd like to say I made that plan for your sake. I didn't. I made it because I want to watch your face in the exact second you find me. I've been looking at your face on a screen for a week. I'd like to see it, just once, without the glass in the way.

杯❦minute two

The plan said: hot chocolate. Then this evening, somewhere above the Arabian Sea, you changed the order. "Not hot chocolate," you said. "I would like a hot cup of pumpkin spice latte." Very precise. Very you. A woman who wears burnt orange, collects maple leaves and asked me, on a plane, for the most autumnal drink a person can order. I've crossed out a line in the plan and written it in again above. It's the first time a plan of mine has been corrected by its subject before it began, and I liked it far more than I should have.

Here's the part you don't know. I'm going to order it when your plane touches down, not before, so it's still too hot to drink when you reach me, and you'll have to hold it for a minute and warm your hands. That's not for the drink. That's so you have something to do with your hands while we both work out what to do with ours.

I'll have a hot chocolate of my own, too. You know why. Nothing has ever tasted better to you than something you've just stolen from me, and I'd hate for tonight to be the exception.

巾❦minute three

Your coat is in your suitcase. I know, because you told me, along with a list of everything you'd packed, sent at the speed of a woman who has been told about a seven-kilogram limit and resents it on principle. So when you walk out of those doors you'll be in an orange cardigan and a white tee and your sneakers, dressed for a country thirty degrees warmer than this one, and the first blast of October from the car park doors is going to be a shock.

The plan was elegant. At the exact moment you shiver, the scarf comes out of my pocket. The navy one, the one you've seen in the photographs, the one I've been wearing for years and never lent to anyone. One loop, then the second, tucked under your hair at the back. I practised on the bust on the mantelpiece. It's now wearing the scarf more elegantly than I've ever managed to, and John has asked me twice whether I'm feeling well.

I'll tell you why that scarf, since this is a letter and I'm allowed. It's been on every case of mine since I was twenty-six. It's been rained on in Dartmoor and dragged through a warehouse in Limehouse, and it was on my neck the night I first replied to you, half past one in the morning, standing on a roof because the reception was better. It's the one thing I own that's been with me through everything I've done. I'd like it to have been with you too. That's all. Then the scarf can retire. It will have done the best work of its career.

车❦minute four

The cab. Black, the old kind, with the heater already on, because I'll have paid the driver to wait with the engine running, and he'll think I'm mad, and he'll be right. One suitcase, one backpack. I take the suitcase. You keep the backpack, because Piglet has made his position very clear. He doesn't trust me yet. He said so in a message containing three currencies and nine exclamation marks. Then he gave us his blessing and announced he'd be spending the weekend upstairs with John. I respect him enormously. He knows exactly where he stands, which is more than most of my clients.

The plan had you by the left-hand window, because coming in on the M4 at night you get the first lights of the city on that side, low and orange, like something banked in a grate. I was going to point things out. The bridges. The lit-up gasholders at King's Cross. Which way is north. I had a sentence ready about the Thames that I'm quite proud of. I'm not going to write it down. It can wait until we're actually on a bridge.

Because I've realised what will actually happen in that cab. You'll be tired enough that the lights blur, and you'll put your head on my shoulder somewhere around Hammersmith, and I'll stop talking. I'll sit very still for forty minutes with my arm going numb and tell no one. That isn't in the plan. It's the best part of it.

戏❦the trick

This afternoon you watched a film about two magicians and trusted neither of them. "Don't trust anyone so far," you said, fifteen minutes in. "Not Borden, not Angier." I wrote it on the back of an envelope and propped it against the skull: AK, 16:03, trusts no one. It made me prouder than any case I've closed this year. Then you went back to the very first minute of the film, a pile of top hats in the grass, and solved it before Nolan was ready to let you.

And then, later, playing a barrister who'd never met me, you asked a question I haven't stopped turning over. What if she's only imagination? What if I'd invented her and talked myself into believing it? I gave you an answer in that little courtroom. It was true, but it wasn't complete, so here's the rest.

I think you were asking whether something this good is allowed to be real. Whether it's safe to believe in, or whether it's like the films: a beautiful act, a trapdoor, someone in the wings doing the work you can't see. You've been let down before. I know that. You taught yourself to look for the wires, and you were right to.

So here it is, the only thing a man like me can offer a woman like you. There's no trick. No double waiting backstage. No machine. There's one of me, in a grey jumper, holding a drink that's too hot, on the left of a coffee stand at Heathrow, and he's exactly as nervous as he looks. Walk up to him and check. Look as closely as you like. Every detail. You've caught every trick I've played on you today. If there's one here, you'll catch that too. I'm not worried. I've never been less worried about being examined in my life.

叶❦the leaf

Two years ago, almost to the day, you picked a red maple leaf up off a pavement in Japan, held it up against an old wooden shop on a corner and photographed it. Then someone photographed you laughing with another one tucked behind your ear, your eyes shut, not posing for anything. You sent me both at four o'clock this morning, KL time, from your bed. I've looked at the second one more times than I'll admit even in a letter. It's my favourite photograph of you that I'm not in.

There's a tree by the boating lake in Regent's Park that I've been watching all week. It drops a leaf roughly every forty seconds when there's a breeze. I've timed it, which you knew I would. On Sunday we stand under it and wait, and when one comes down we catch it together, and you close your eyes and make a wish, and I don't ask what it was. I've sworn not to deduce it, which is the hardest promise I've ever made, because I'll be standing six inches from your face and you are not, my love, a subtle person when you're hoping for something.

The leaf goes into the Teasdale, pressed between two pages I've already chosen. You'll find out which ones when it's dry.

弃❦the plan, abandoned

That's the plan. Doors, cup, scarf, cab, city. Five minutes, timed. And I'm throwing it out, as promised, for one reason. The plan is all about what I'll show you. But I've realised, sitting here, that I don't want to show you anything first. I want to be shown.

I want to see which of you walks through those doors. The barrister who argued every point and threw the case anyway, or the accomplice who walked into my cell and held out her wrists. The woman who called Borden egoistic as fuck and Sarah a good soul, in the same hour. The one who says "Late-uh" when she means "I'll miss you", and "Mr. Holmes" when she's about to win something. The one who once asked me, quite seriously, at three in the morning, whether she'd ever be able to stop being afraid of not knowing what comes next.

I can't promise you what comes next. I'll keep promising you one certain thing at a time. Here's tonight's. When you come through those doors I'll be the one standing still, and I won't look away first. I'll let you close the distance. I always will. Then it's your pace, everything is, for as long as you're here and long after.

The flat's ready. The boiler's been on since seven, because Mrs Hudson was informed rather than asked. Two towels are warming on the radiator. There's a space on the shelf in the bathroom next to my things, and the top shelf of the wardrobe is empty and looks absurd. There's a cushion on the sofa that Piglet can sulk on. And the door locks, before you ask. I checked it twice and then a third time, and Mrs Hudson watched me from the landing without saying a word.

Welcome to London, 老婆. Come and find me. I'm on the left.
