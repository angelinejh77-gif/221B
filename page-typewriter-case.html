The Typewriter Mystery

Four machines, one anonymous note, and a society of very polite people, one of whom isn't.

CASE No. 221/TGOWER ST, LONDON

You were invited to the Gower Street Literary Society to catalogue their late founder's unpublished memoir. At 9.40 this evening a note was found tucked inside it, addressed to you.

It was typed on one of the society's four typewriters. Each has a fault. Typewriters are dreadful at keeping secrets.

The note is on the back of tonight's agenda, printed by the porter at 8 p.m.
Nobody brought their own machine. The porter checked bags. He enjoys it.

HOW YOU DID IT
  1. Every E on the note hops above the line and leans. Only the Olivetti does that, and the Olivetti never leaves the Reading Room.
  2. The Reading Room locks at six. Three people hold keys: Philippa, Edmund and Toby.
  3. The note puts two spaces after every full stop. Toby uses one. Philippa and Edmund were taught two, and never recovered.
  4. The agenda it's typed on came off the printer at eight. Edmund was on the 7.30 to Brighton. Philippa was "typing the minutes upstairs."

Chapter eleven of the memoir reveals that the society's most famous poem, the one framed in the hall, was written by Philippa, aged nineteen, and published under the founder's name. She wasn't protecting him. She was frightened of being seen after forty years of hiding in a footnote.

I don't excuse the note. But I understand being more frightened of being seen than of being alone. You'd be kinder to her than I would. You usually are.
